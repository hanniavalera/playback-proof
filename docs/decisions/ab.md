# The A/B harness decisions (ADR-140 to ADR-149)

Architecture decision records for the harness that compares two player configurations: the statistics in [`ab/stats`](../../ab/stats), the pre-registration ([`ab/prereg.ts`](../../ab/prereg.ts)), the runner ([`ab/experiment.ts`](../../ab/experiment.ts), [`ab/execute.ts`](../../ab/execute.ts)), the analysis and the decision rule ([`ab/analyze.ts`](../../ab/analyze.ts), [`ab/decision.ts`](../../ab/decision.ts)), the validity of a run ([`ab/validity.ts`](../../ab/validity.ts)), the A/A calibration and the power analysis ([`ab/aa.ts`](../../ab/aa.ts), [`ab/power.ts`](../../ab/power.ts), [`ab/pilot.ts`](../../ab/pilot.ts)), the report ([`ab/report.ts`](../../ab/report.ts)), the policy numbers in [`ab/policy.ts`](../../ab/policy.ts) and the command `npm run ab` ([`ab/run.ts`](../../ab/run.ts), [`ab/cli.ts`](../../ab/cli.ts)). The policy it implements is [TEST_STRATEGY.md](../../TEST_STRATEGY.md) section 12 and the catalog rows PB-AB-001 (the A/A check), PB-AB-002 (startup under `high-latency`) and PB-AB-003 (rebuffering under `bandwidth-drop`); the metrics are defined in [METRICS.md](../METRICS.md); the two configurations are ADR-064 in [player.md](player.md); the one-browser-process-per-batch policy (ADR-120) and the percentile definition (ADR-121) are in [perf.md](perf.md); the command, its files and its exit codes are section 9.2 of [CONTRACTS.md](../CONTRACTS.md).

**Where the numbers come from.** Phase 6 has two parts. Part 1, the one these records describe, built the harness and **ran no real experiment**: every figure quoted below comes from arithmetic, from a published example, from an independent implementation (scipy, through committed vectors: [`ab/vectors`](../../ab/vectors)), or from synthetic sessions in the unit tests (and each says which test pins it). The only real run of Part 1 is the **pipeline check**: six sessions of one condition, on a workstation shared with other work, kept in [`docs/results/ab/pipeline-check/`](../results/ab/README.md) and labelled in every line as plumbing only, not evidence. Nothing (no threshold, size, margin or level) was set from it. Part 2 runs the pilots and the experiments, and its results are in `docs/results/ab/`; the claims below that depend on them say "part 2 measures".

**What the first real run showed.** The whole path worked on the first run: the player page built, the service started as a child process, one Chromium ran six sessions with the arms interleaved (A B, B A, A B), the page proved the configuration of every arm, the service proved the registered rules and counted the requests each fired on (19 for each session of A, 28 to 30 for each session of B), the git provenance of the plan was read, the report was written with a validity table of 6 of 6 plumbing checks, and `--report` regenerated it byte for byte. It also found what no fake could: the report printed the content hash of **every** stream the service holds instead of the one in use (fixed, with a test whose fixture now holds two streams); the progress lines showed the startup time with the noise of a float (828.8999999761581 ms; whole milliseconds now); and a run interrupted by SIGTERM printed a confusing failure of the session that was in flight (the browser was closing under it), so the signal handler now says that an interrupted run writes nothing, and the exit code is 130. After the interrupt the service, the browser and the port were gone (checked on the real processes).

## ADR-140: The effect is a Hodges-Lehmann shift with a seeded bootstrap interval, and Cliff's delta stands beside it

**Context.** The two metrics the catalog asks for have awkward shapes. Startup time is right-skewed (a few slow sessions); the rebuffer count and ratio are zero in most sessions. With thirty sessions per arm, a difference of means is moved by one slow session, and a t-interval assumes a shape the data do not have.

**Decision.** The effect of B against A on a continuous metric is the **Hodges-Lehmann shift**: the median of all `n1 n2` differences `b - a` ([`hodges-lehmann.ts`](../../ab/stats/hodges-lehmann.ts); a selection algorithm on the 900 differences, not a sort). Its interval is a **seeded percentile bootstrap**: each arm is resampled on its own with replacement, the shift of the resample is taken, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 5000 of them (never fewer than 1000 in a registered experiment) are the interval, with the type-7 quantile definition of ADR-121. The seed is in the pre-registration, so the report can be produced again, byte for byte, from the raw results. **Cliff's delta**, `(#(b > a) - #(b < a)) / (n1 n2)`, goes beside it as a scale-free summary with the usual labels (negligible below 0.147, small below 0.33, medium below 0.474, then large, Romano 2006); it informs the reader and decides nothing.

It is tested for what it is: a median of pairwise differences on two worked examples; equal to the full sort of every difference on 150 random samples with ties; equivariant (shifting B by `c` shifts the shift and the whole interval by `c`, scaling by `k` scales them, swapping the arms negates them, with the same draws); not moved by one extreme session where the difference of means is; and covering the true shift about 95 % of the time over 150 simulated experiments of 30 against 30. Against an independent implementation (ADR-141): the shift and Cliff's delta equal numpy's `median(subtract.outer(b, a))` and `sign(subtract.outer(b, a)).mean()` on ten pairs of samples with and without ties, and the bootstrap interval agrees with `scipy.stats.bootstrap(method='percentile')` to within 5 % of its width on three data sets (a different generator, so it can only agree up to Monte Carlo error: the largest difference seen was 1.1 % of the width; resampling one arm only, or taking the 5th and 95th percentiles, fails all three).

**Alternatives considered.** Welch's t-test on the means (a shape assumption, and an outlier decides it). The difference of the two medians (unstable when the data have ties, and it is zero for a count that is zero in most sessions). A BCa interval (needs an acceleration constant from the jackknife of 900 differences for no demonstrated gain: the coverage check is what would show the need).

**Consequences.** For a metric that is zero in most sessions the shift stays at 0 until B exceeds A in more than half of all the pairs, which takes a large change, and its interval collapses to a point: that is not a precise estimate, it is a degenerate one, and the report says so in the row (a warning in the comparison). That is why the decision about rebuffering rests on the **share of sessions that stalled at all** (ADR-142) and the count and the ratio are reported beside it. The interval describes the sampling noise of these sessions on this machine: it is not an interval for members.

**Defending it.** "The data are skewed and full of zeros, so I did not use a statistic that needs them to be neither. The shift is the median of every B-minus-A difference, the interval is a resample of my own sessions with a seed, and every property I rely on has a test that fails if I break it."

## ADR-141: Mann-Whitney is exact where that is cheap and where ties are heavy, and the A/A calibration is why

**Context.** The p-value has to answer "does a session of B tend to be larger than a session of A" without assuming a shape. The brief's rule was: exact when both arms have at most 12 sessions, the normal approximation with a tie correction otherwise. The first calibration of the harness (ADR-148) on synthetic pools of identical sessions showed that this rule is not safe for a metric that is zero in most sessions.

**Decision.** `mannWhitneyU` ([`mann-whitney.ts`](../../ab/stats/mann-whitney.ts)) reports `U1 = #(a > b) + 1/2 #(a = b)` (the convention of `scipy.stats.mannwhitneyu`) and two p-values. The **exact** one is the probability, over all `C(N, n1)` assignments of the pooled sessions to arm A with the observed ties held fixed, of a rank sum at least as far from its mean as the observed one; it is computed by a dynamic programme over the groups of tied values, so it is exact with ties (and equals the classical distribution when there are none) and takes a few milliseconds for 30 against 30. The **asymptotic** one is `z = (|U1 - n1 n2 / 2| - 1/2) / sigma` with the tie-corrected variance `n1 n2 / 12 * [(N + 1) - sum (t^3 - t) / (N (N - 1))]` and `p = erfc(z / sqrt 2)`. `auto` is exact when both arms have at most 12 sessions **or when ties are heavy**: on average three or more pooled sessions share a value, and the programme is cheap (pooled sample at most 400, at most 60 million additions). The result says which method produced it, and the report prints it for every metric.

The deviation from the brief is the last clause, and the evidence is one test: a synthetic A/A pool of 60 sessions whose rebuffer count is 0 in 50 of them, split at random into two arms of 30, 1000 times. The normal approximation declared the identical arms different in **78 splits (7.8 %)** at alpha 5 %, the exact distribution in **34 (3.4 %)**; the test pins both numbers. The rank sum of such data lives on a coarse lattice (it moves in steps of the ranks of the few non-zero sessions), and a continuity correction of 1/2 is far too small for it. Over 60 zero-inflated samples of 30 against 30 the exact test took a median of 3.6 ms (p90 4.3 ms, one thread, on a machine shared with other work).

**How it is checked.** Against an enumeration of every assignment for pooled samples up to 22 sessions, with and without ties (p-value, variance and distribution); against the published 5 % two-sided critical values of U for `n = 4` to `12` (0, 2, 5, 8, 13, 17, 23, 30, 37) and the worked illustration of the encyclopedia article on the test (six tortoises and six hares: U = 11 and 25); against a hand calculation of `z` on tied data; against a permutation test of 20 000 draws on a zero-inflated sample (within 0.015 of the exact p); and by properties (the order inside an arm, swapping the arms, a monotone transform of the data change nothing). Flipping the sign of the tie correction fails five of these tests.

**Against an independent implementation.** The statistics were written here so that every p-value can be traced to code in the repository, which also means they were written by the same hand as the tests that pin them. So [`ab/vectors/generate.py`](../../ab/vectors/generate.py) asks scipy 1.17.1 and numpy 2.4.6 the same questions on the same inputs (fixed seeds, the versions written into the file) and commits the answers as [`scipy-vectors.json`](../../ab/vectors/scipy-vectors.json); [`scipy-vectors.test.ts`](../../tests/unit/ab/scipy-vectors.test.ts) compares the TypeScript with them within stated tolerances, and **python is in no gate and no CI job**: the fixture is data and the script documents how it was made. They cover `erfc`, the normal cdf and quantile; U and the two-sided p of the test in its four forms (exact without ties against `mannwhitneyu(method='exact')`; the continuity-corrected approximation without and with ties against `mannwhitneyu(method='asymptotic')`; exact with ties against `permutation_test` over every assignment, because scipy's exact method ignores ties); Fisher's test on 54 tables; the Wilson and Clopper-Pearson intervals at three levels on 19 counts; Newcombe's interval (not in scipy: assembled in the script from scipy's Wilson intervals and checked against the paper's own example); the shift, Cliff's delta, the type-7 quantile and the bootstrap interval (ADR-140). Apart from the bootstrap interval (a Monte Carlo figure), every figure agrees with scipy to at worst 5e-14 relative (erfc, the normal tail, the asymptotic p) or 1.5e-12 absolute (Clopper-Pearson, a bisection on a binomial cdf), except the normal quantile far out in the tail (1.1e-11: a double cannot say more there); U agrees exactly, and so do the shift and Cliff's delta. Holm has no scipy counterpart and keeps its worked example from the literature (ADR-142). The check does not say the statistics are right in every case: it says that where an independent implementation exists, the two agree on those inputs.

**Mutations, each caught.** Dropping the continuity correction fails 5 tests (3 of the older ones and 2 vectors), dropping the correction for ties 5, the tail of the exact p taken one step off 22 (three of them vectors, one for each form of the exact test), Fisher's tail counted with `<` instead of `<=` 6, a wrong Wilson centre 6 and a wrong end of Newcombe's interval 4, the lower Clopper-Pearson tail one event off 3, the difference taken as `a - b` 12, Cliff's delta with the opposite sign 5, a quantile of type 6 2, the continued fraction of erfc cut at depth 6 15; a bootstrap that resamples one arm only, or takes a 90 % interval, 4 each (the three bootstrap vectors and the coverage test).

**Alternatives considered.** The approximation alone (anti-conservative on ties: that is the finding). A Monte Carlo permutation p-value (random, so two runs of the same report could differ, and noisy near the level; the exact one is deterministic and fast enough). Dropping the continuity correction (it makes the approximation more eager, not less).

**Consequences.** The exact p-value is conditional on the observed pattern of ties, as every permutation test is. Beyond the size and cost caps `auto` falls back to the approximation, and the method is recorded so a reader sees it. A forced `exact` above the caps is refused.

**Defending it.** "I did not take the textbook rule on trust. I tested the harness on sessions that were identical by construction, found that it cried wolf 7.8 % of the time on a metric full of zeros, and changed the test until it did not. The numbers are in a test that fails if they move."

## ADR-142: One family decides, Holm adjusts it, and for rebuffering a share decides

**Context.** Every session records every metric (METRICS.md). Comparing all of them and publishing the one that is "significant" is how an experiment finds what it wants. And two metrics decide here, startup and rebuffering, so the chance that at least one of them is a false alarm is above alpha unless something corrects for it.

**Decision.** A pre-registration names its metrics and gives each a role. **Primary** metrics form the family: their p-values are adjusted with **Holm's step-down method** ([`holm.ts`](../../ab/stats/holm.ts); the adjusted value is the running maximum of `(m - rank) p`, so it controls the family-wise error rate under any dependence and is never less powerful than Bonferroni) and they alone enter the decision rule. **Secondary** metrics are pre-registered and reported with their raw p-values, marked as a description, and never decide. Every other metric the sessions recorded is compared in an **exploratory** section with raw p-values and no threshold, headed by the sentence that one in twenty of them will look significant by chance.

For rebuffering the primary metric is **the share of sessions with at least one rebuffer** (derived from the count). The test is **Fisher's exact test** (two-sided, probabilities compared exactly with big integers, up to 1000 sessions); the effect is the difference of shares, B minus A, with **Newcombe's hybrid score interval** (Wilson score intervals of the two arms combined, method 10 of Newcombe 1998), and each arm's own share has its Wilson interval. The count, the total time and the ratio are secondary. Holm reproduces the worked example of the literature (0.01, 0.04, 0.03 and 0.005 adjust to 0.03, 0.06, 0.06 and 0.02), is the identity for one p-value, and holds the family-wise error rate in simulation under the global null; Newcombe reproduces the paper's own example (56 of 70 against 48 of 80: a difference of 0.2 with the interval 0.0524 to 0.3339); Fisher reproduces the tea-tasting table (34 / 70) and a table of the R documentation.

**Alternatives considered.** No adjustment (the family-wise error rate with three metrics is about 14 %). Bonferroni (valid, less powerful). Benjamini-Hochberg (controls the share of false discoveries among the rejected, not the chance of any: for a go or no-go on shipping a configuration the second is what is wanted). A single metric (loses the "not worse on the other" half of the rule). The count of rebuffers as the decision metric (its shift is degenerate, ADR-140).

**Consequences.** A metric that is secondary or exploratory and looks like a win is a hypothesis for the next pre-registration, never a finding of this one. A share needs many sessions to tell a small difference: from a baseline of no stalls at all, thirty sessions per arm find a rise to about a quarter of the sessions 80 % of the time (a test checks the figure against the exact power of Fisher's test), and from a baseline of a quarter they need more. ADR-147 puts the number in the pre-registration before the run.

**Defending it.** "Two questions decide, so I paid for asking two. The ones that do not decide are on the page, but they are labelled as what they are."

## ADR-143: A pre-registration is a validated file, hashed and committed before the run, and the runner refuses to run without it

**Context.** An experiment whose metric, threshold or number of sessions can be changed after the data are in proves nothing. What has to be shown is that the plan existed, as it is, before the data did.

**Decision.** The plan is a JSON file in [`ab/prereg/`](../../ab/prereg) (schema version 1). [`prereg.ts`](../../ab/prereg.ts) validates it strictly and reports every problem at once: an unknown field is an error (a typo in a threshold must not become a threshold that silently is not there); an experiment needs at least thirty sessions per arm and an even number (ADR-144), alpha 0.05, at least 1000 bootstrap resamples, one condition, and for every primary metric a practical threshold with its stated basis and the **minimum detectable effect** of a committed pilot at the same number of sessions with at least 80 % power, with the threshold not below it (ADR-147). A condition is written out as fault rules, checked with the service's own validator; if it names a profile, the rules must equal the current profile; a fault that starts after the observation window is an error; a stretch of the session in which the player cannot hear the network is an **error** for every kind but the pipeline check, which only warns (an experiment runs on finite links only: ADR-149); and no free-text field of a plan (the title, the hypotheses, the rationales, the text of the rules, the basis of a threshold) may name a single setting of configuration B, because B is several settings together and the comparison is of the two configurations as shipped (ADR-149). An experiment also names, in `aaCheck.source`, the committed report of the A/A run in which the harness passed its own check in this environment; the runner reads it before the first session (ADR-148). There are four kinds: `experiment` (decides), `exploratory` (labelled, estimates, decides nothing), `aa` (both arms run configuration A) and `pipeline-check` (a few sessions that prove the plumbing; decides nothing and may break the size rules).

`npm run ab -- --prereg <file>` loads and validates the file, asks git whether it is tracked, unmodified and committed (and which commit and when), and **refuses to run** (exit 2) otherwise; `--allow-uncommitted-prereg` runs anyway, and the report then says in words that it does not prove the plan came first. The raw results file embeds the **exact text** of the plan, its **sha256** and the git provenance; the analysis recomputes the hash, refuses a file whose plan no longer matches, and re-validates the embedded plan, so a report is a function of the raw results (and of the calibrations written beside them) and `--report` regenerates it, byte for byte, without a browser (a test runs it). A plan that changes after the data is a new file with a new id, reported next to the first.

**What it proves, and what it does not.** It proves that a commit containing this exact plan is dated before the run started, and that the results were analysed with that plan. It does not prove that the commit was not rewritten locally before it was shared: the public history of the repository is the witness for that, and the report says which commit it saw.

**Alternatives considered.** A plan in an issue or a comment (not machine-checked, not byte-exact). Only the hash in the results (the plan could not be re-validated from the results alone). A signed commit (more machinery than the claim needs). A schema with defaults for the missing fields (a default is a decision nobody wrote down).

**Consequences.** Writing a pre-registration is slower than running an experiment, which is the point. The pipeline check and the A/A kinds exist so that the plumbing can be verified without pretending to be an experiment.

**Defending it.** "The plan is a file, the file is in a commit, the commit is older than the data, and the results carry the hash. If I changed my mind after seeing the data, the report says so."

## ADR-144: The arms are interleaved in blocks of two, and the arm that goes first alternates

**Context.** The machine drifts: other processes, the thermal state, the OS caches. Running all of A and then all of B turns a drift into an effect of the configuration.

**Decision.** [`blockSchedule`](../../ab/schedule.ts) makes a **block** of one session of each arm, and the arm that goes first alternates (A B, B A, A B, ...). The first `warmupSessionsPerArm` blocks are the **warm-up**: they are run exactly like the others, **recorded** with every number, and **left out** of every statistic (ADR-120). With an even number of measured blocks both arms have the same mean position in time, so a linear drift cancels exactly (a test checks equal mean positions for 2, 4 and 30 blocks, and that an odd number does not balance, which is why the validator wants an even `sessionsPerArm`). The load average is recorded before and after every condition, and the report says when the machine was busy (more than half its CPUs, the threshold of the performance report).

**Alternatives considered.** Random order (balanced only in expectation: with thirty sessions per arm a chance imbalance stays). Alternating without blocks (A B A B puts A always first in its pair, so a "second session is slower" effect lands on B). All of A then all of B (the drift is the effect).

**Consequences.** The design cancels a linear drift and spreads a slow wave evenly; it does not protect against an interference with a period of exactly four sessions, which the recorded load average and the per-session start times would show. Two sessions never run at once: an experiment is serial, and takes as long as its sessions do.

**Defending it.** "I did not trust randomness to balance thirty sessions. The order is a schedule with a proof that it cancels a drift, and a test of the proof."

## ADR-145: One browser per condition, a fresh context and service session per session, the arm's configuration proved by the page, and failures recorded

**Context.** An A/B comparison compares what ran, so the harness must show that arm B ran configuration B, and every session that did not finish must be counted, not forgotten.

**Decision.** One condition is one **browser process** (as a performance batch, ADR-120), and every session is a **fresh context** and a **service session of its own** carrying the condition's rules, created before and deleted after, also when the session fails. After the page opens, `qa.info()` must say that the configuration is the arm's letter, that the overrides it applied are canonically equal to `configFor(letter)`, and that ABR is on; at the end the load mode must be Media Source Extensions. A mismatch is a defect of the harness (`HarnessError`): the run ends and nothing partial is passed off as a result. The **condition is proved the same way**, from the service and not from the plan: after the session is created the runner reads back the rules the service holds for it (canonically equal to the registered rules, without their ids, or the run ends), and after the session it reads the request log and counts, for every rule, the requests that rule fired on. That count goes into the raw results (`applied`); a rule that fired on no request means the condition was not applied in that session, which makes the run not valid (ADR-146) however the numbers look, and the count is why a report can say "every rule fired on at least 19 requests of the session" (the sentence of the pipeline check) and not just "the rules were sent". A run interrupted by SIGINT or SIGTERM stops the service and the browser and **writes nothing** (exit 130): a partial result would invite stopping when the data look good. A session that reports a critical error, times out or loses its page is **recorded as failed** with the kind, the message and the player's own diagnostics, with no metrics, and it is never retried (a configuration that fails more often must show it). A condition stops, keeping what it has and the reason, after `abortAfterConsecutiveFailures` failures in a row: the harness is broken, not the player. The **failure guard** of ADR-146 reads the failed counts.

**Alternatives considered.** Retrying failed sessions (hides the difference between arms). A page per session in one context (the cache and the connection pool would carry over). Trusting the URL parameter (the page proves the configuration, not the URL).

**Consequences.** Each session costs a browser context, which is what a member's visit costs. The tests use a fake browser, service and player: what they pin is when things are created, recorded, discarded and closed, which arm runs with which configuration, and what becomes of a failure; what a real browser does is the pipeline check.

**Defending it.** "The page tells me what it ran, and a run that cannot prove it stops. A failure is data: it goes in the report, with the arm it happened in."

## ADR-146: The decision rule has four verdicts, never conflated; the lab passes an experiment that was valid, whatever the direction

**Context.** TEST_STRATEGY.md section 12 fixes the rule in words. A rule that is applied by a person after the data are in is a rule that bends; this one is a function. And two different questions get confused unless they are kept apart: *what did the experiment find?* (the verdict) and *can the finding be trusted?* (validity). A lab that "passes" an experiment because B won would be grading the player; one that fails it because B lost would be punishing an honest result.

**Decision: four verdicts.** [`decide`](../../ab/decision.ts) (rule id `pb-ab-v1`) puts every primary metric on one scale where positive means B is worse, with `worst` and `best` the two ends of its 95 % interval and `d` its practical threshold, and reads four statements: **better** (adjusted p below alpha and `worst < -d`), **harmful** (the effect beyond `d` and `best > 0`), **harmless** (`worst < d`) and **equivalent** (`best > -d` and `worst < d`). Then, in this order, one of four verdicts, each driving one action:

| Verdict | When | Drives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A better** | any primary metric is harmful: B is worse than the smallest effect worth acting on and the interval excludes zero (harm wins over any benefit elsewhere, and the sentence names the benefit it does not buy) | do not ship B |
| **B better** | at least one metric is better and all are harmless: the whole interval is beyond the threshold in B's favour, adjusted p below alpha, and no other primary metric can be worse than its threshold | ship B |
| **Equivalent within the margin** | every primary metric is equivalent: the whole interval lies inside plus or minus the margin | keep A; **the only verdict that may say "no meaningful difference"** |
| **Inconclusive** | anything else: the interval is too wide to decide | keep A and collect more sessions: the report says how many more per arm the observed spread would need |

Every comparison is strict: a bound exactly on a threshold is not beyond it. **Absence of significance is not evidence of equivalence**: a p-value above alpha with a wide interval is inconclusive, and the sentence says "not finding a difference is not evidence that there is none". A test walks every sentence the rule can produce (headline, reason, steps, the reading of each metric) and fails if any but the equivalent verdict contains "no (meaningful) difference".

**One number is both the smallest effect worth acting on and the margin of equivalence**: the practical threshold `d` of the plan, with its stated basis, never below the minimum detectable effect of the pilot (ADR-147). A second number would be a second thing to defend, and what the design can see bounds both.

**The burden of proof is asymmetric on purpose, as section 12 writes it.** B better needs the interval *entirely* beyond the threshold, significance and no possible harm; A better needs only a point estimate beyond the threshold and an interval that excludes zero. So an improvement whose interval excludes zero and whose point estimate is beyond the threshold, but whose near end is inside the threshold, is **inconclusive**, not "B better", and the report says how many more sessions would settle it. A symmetric reading ("the interval excludes zero and the effect exceeds the threshold", for both directions) would have called it B better; section 12 is stricter for shipping, and this rule follows section 12.

**The guard.** A session that failed has no metrics, so a comparison of the survivors is trustworthy only if few failed and the arms failed alike. The guard trips when an arm's failed share exceeds the pre-registered maximum (10 % unless it says otherwise) or when Fisher's test finds the failed counts of the arms different. A tripped guard forbids "B better" and "equivalent" and leaves "A better" alone: a harm found among the survivors is still a harm. Exploratory and pipeline-check plans have no decision.

**Decision: validity, not direction, is what the lab passes.** [`assessValidity`](../../ab/validity.ts) applies seven gates to a run, each with its result and what was checked in the report, and the run is **valid** when every one passes:

| Gate | Passes when |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-registration fixed first | the embedded plan hashes to the recorded sha256 and git says the file was committed unmodified, before the run started (ADR-143) |
| Design followed | the sessions are exactly the plan's schedule (blocks of two, the first arm alternating, the warm-up blocks first: [`blockSchedule`](../../ab/schedule.ts), ADR-144), the rules and the stream are the plan's, every condition ran and none stopped early |
| Configuration proved | every finished session's page reported its arm's letter, overrides canonically equal to the arm's, and played through Media Source Extensions (ADR-145) |
| Condition applied, the same in both arms | the service held exactly the registered rules in every session, and every rule fired on at least one request (ADR-145) |
| Failures few and even | the failure guard is not tripped |
| Machine quiet at the start | the 1-minute load average before the first session was at most half the CPUs |
| A/A check passed | the committed report named by `aaCheck.source` is valid, of this environment and engine, and its real A/A and calibration passed for this condition (ADR-148) |

The summary line says "VALID: ... The direction of the result does not enter: it drives the decision named for the row" or "NOT VALID: <the gates>. The verdict below must not be acted on, whichever way it points". The **exit code follows validity** (CONTRACTS section 9.2): 0 for a valid run with any of the four verdicts, 2 for a run that is not valid; an A/A run that is valid but in which the harness failed its own check exits 1. A **pipeline check** is never evidence: its gates are labelled plumbing checks, the machine gate is not enforced, it prints no verdict, and its summary says "plumbing only, not evidence".

**Where this differs from the catalog.** The "what a failure drives" cells of PB-AB-002 and PB-AB-003 in TEST_STRATEGY.md read as if B being slower (or stalling more) were the failure of the test, and drive "do not ship B"; here that is the verdict "A better", a valid and passing result of the experiment that drives the same action. The catalog rows are generated and were not changed; the difference is in the words "failure" and "pass", and is reported to whoever owns the catalog.

**Tests.** The boundary (an interval whose worse end is exactly the threshold is not beyond it), harm before benefit, the guard, the vocabulary rule, the actions, and each gate failing on its own with every verdict still "valid" when the gates pass. Mutations, each caught: the "better" comparison made non-strict (1 test), harm no longer winning over a benefit elsewhere (2), the guard ignored when shipping (4), the equivalence read from the point estimate instead of the whole interval (9), B better without every metric harmless (1), the inconclusive headline saying "no meaningful difference" (4, among them the vocabulary test), no significant benefit counted as equivalent (14), the equivalent verdict driving "ship B" (2), a run called not valid when A is better (3), an invalid run exiting 0 (5), the condition gate ignoring a rule that never fired (2), the machine gate always passing (2), the A/A gate ignoring a failed A/A (1), the design gate not checking positions (1), the configuration gate not comparing overrides (1). One mutation **survived** the first suite: an A/A verdict of "A better" or "B better" without a significant adjusted p was counted as acceptable; a test now pins that a verdict that declares a difference between two copies of A is a false positive too.

**Alternatives considered.** Three outcomes (merged two situations: data too weak to say, and data that positively show nothing worth a change). A two one-sided test for equivalence (a looser claim than the strict one, and a different interval from the one the table shows). A rule with a stopping point in the middle (it would need a sequential design; the sessions are fixed in advance). Passing the lab on the direction of the result (grades the player, not the experiment).

**Defending it.** "There are four things an experiment can find and I wrote each one down with the action it drives. 'No difference' is a finding only when the whole interval sits inside the margin; a wide interval is inconclusive and says how much more it would take. And the lab passes a run for being trustworthy, not for what it found."

## ADR-147: The practical threshold is never below what the design can tell from noise, and the number comes from simulation on an A/A pilot

**Context.** A threshold the design cannot detect is a decision about differences that cannot be seen. And the number of sessions per arm has to be chosen before the run, from the variance the harness really has.

**Decision.** [`power.ts`](../../ab/power.ts) takes the shape from the pilot, not from a formula: a **simulated experiment** draws `n` sessions with replacement from the sessions of configuration A under the condition for each arm, shifts arm B (added to every value; for a share, sessions are flipped so that B's share moves by the shift), and runs the test of the harness on them. **Power** at a shift is the share of simulated experiments the test finds different. The **minimum detectable effect** is the smallest shift whose power reaches 80 %, by doubling and then 20 bisections, in both directions (a skewed pilot is easier to move one way), taking the larger; it is `null`, with the reason, when even the largest possible shift is found less often. Every simulated experiment has its own seeded stream, the same for every shift (common random numbers), so the power curve is smooth. `sessionsForPower` gives the even `n` at which a shift is found 80 % of the time. The validator refuses a registered threshold below the pilot's minimum, or one computed for another `n` or another power.

`verdictRates` goes further and runs the **whole pipeline** (test, bootstrap interval, rule) on simulated experiments at a true shift of zero, of minus the threshold and of plus the threshold, because detecting is not deciding. At a true shift equal to the threshold the rule says "B better" about one time in forty, because the far end of a 95 % interval lies beyond the true value only that often. The same holds for "equivalent within the margin": for normal data with a true shift of zero it needs the threshold at about 3.2 standard errors (a 95 % interval inside the band, 80 % of the time) against 2.8 for the minimum detectable effect, and for a share of 0.25 with thirty sessions per arm the 95 % interval of a difference is about plus or minus 0.22, so a threshold of 0.2 can never be shown equivalent whatever the sessions do (it can only be inconclusive). `npm run ab -- --power` prints these columns, one per verdict, for a pilot before anyone writes a threshold.

**How it is checked.** Against the exact power of Fisher's test for two binomials (simulation agrees within its Monte Carlo interval), against the two-sample theory (for normal data the minimum detectable effect at thirty per arm is about 0.74 standard deviations with the efficiency of the rank test), for monotonicity in the shift and in `n`, and for reproducibility from the seed.

**Alternatives considered.** A closed-form power formula (assumes the shape that ADR-140 rejects). A threshold chosen first and the sessions set from it with no validation (the pilot is what says whether the threshold is meaningful). Sequential stopping when the interval looks good (inflates the false-positive rate; the sessions are fixed).

**Consequences.** The threshold is a choice of the person who registers the plan and has to be defended in words (`thresholdBasis`); the minimum only forbids the indefensible. If the pilot says the design cannot reach the threshold, the answer is more sessions or a different question, and part 2 reports which.

**Defending it.** "The threshold is the smallest difference worth changing a default for, and I am not allowed to set it where the harness cannot see. The pilot says where that is, before I write the number."

## ADR-148: The A/A calibration comes first, in two forms, and it has a rule for passing

**Context.** Every verdict rests on the false-positive rate of the harness: how often it finds a difference between two copies of the same configuration. If that is above alpha, no verdict is worth publishing (the catalog row, and TEST_STRATEGY.md section 12).

**Decision.** There are two checks, because each sees what the other cannot.

*(a) Random splits of a pool* ([`calibrate`](../../ab/aa.ts)): a pool of sessions of configuration A is split at random into two equal arms (an odd session is left out), 2000 times (the policy), and **the full pipeline** (`analyzeCondition`, the code that produces a verdict) is applied to each split. Every split compares two samples of one configuration, so every significant one is a false positive. The calibration **passes when the lower end of the exact 95 % interval of the false-positive rate is not above alpha**, that is, when the data do not show the rate to be above 5 %; with 2000 splits and a true rate of 5 % the observed rate has a spread of half a point, so "the rate is at most 5 %" taken literally would fail a healthy harness half the time. The report also gives how the rule's verdicts came out over the splits, the rejection rate of every primary metric at four levels (a valid test has each at most its level), and says when the rate is well below alpha (safe, conservative, and its power is what pays). It describes the splits drawn, not the pool, since they share their sessions, and a random split mixes away the order of the sessions, so a drift of the machine over time is invisible to it.

*(b) The real interleaved A/A* (kind `aa`): both arms run configuration A through the runner, in blocks, and the report reads like any other. The harness has passed when nothing is significant after Holm and the verdict is neither "A better" nor "B better" (between two copies of A, either is a false positive, even when the adjusted p-value alone does not say so: a test pins that); the ideal reading is "equivalent within the margin", and "inconclusive" is acceptable but not ideal (the design is too weak to say there is no difference: not finding one is not evidence that there is none). The run exits **1** when the harness fails (the A/A check failed, not a plumbing error), and **2** when the A/A run itself is not valid. One run is significant 5 % of the time with a perfect harness: a failure is a reason to repeat it as a new pre-registered run and to read the calibration, and two in a row are a defect of the harness.

**The experiment relies on it, mechanically.** The pre-registration of an experiment names, in `aaCheck.source`, the committed report (`docs/results/ab/<name>.report.json`) of the A/A run that was made for it. Before the first session the runner reads that file and **stops the machine, exit 2, if** it is missing, is not a report of an A/A run, was measured in another environment or with another engine (the check is per environment: a drift of the machine is a property of the machine), is not valid itself, or if its real A/A or its calibration of the experiment's condition did not pass. The same check is the last gate of validity (ADR-146), so the report of the experiment says whether the harness had passed its own test, and the A/A report it relied on.

**The calibration can fail.** Its own tests give it a pipeline that finds differences too often (it fails, with the sentence that no verdict is published), a pipeline that forgets Holm (three primary metrics, each at alpha, raise the family-wise rate above it: it fails, and the honest one passes), and a pipeline that almost never finds anything (it passes and is called conservative). Removing Holm from the pipeline fails eight tests, among them the calibration's own.

**What it found.** The normal approximation of the rank test was too eager on a metric that is zero in most sessions (78 of 1000 splits, ADR-141), and the harness changed because of it. What the real A/A of part 2 finds is in `docs/results/ab/`.

**Alternatives considered.** Passing when the point estimate is at most alpha (fails healthy harnesses). Fewer splits (with a few hundred the interval is about two points wide on each side, too wide to tell 5 % from 7 %). Only the real A/A (one run says little about a rate of 5 %). Only splits (blind to ordering and to the machine).

**Defending it.** "Before I let the harness say B is better than A, I made it say that A is different from A, 2000 times, and counted. It cried wolf, so I fixed it. Then I ran it on the real thing."

## ADR-149: A condition is a finite link, and an experiment says what it cannot say

**Context.** Shaka 5.3.3's bandwidth estimator ignores a download that took less than 5 ms (`abr.cacheLoadThreshold`) and treats it as a cache hit (ADR-095 in [e2e-abr.md](e2e-abr.md)). On the lab's origin with no rule, a 75 kB segment arrives in a fraction of a millisecond, so whether an engine's estimate hears of the network at all depends on how fast its fetch path is: a property of the machine, not of the configuration. An experiment that compares two ABR configurations must not rest on that.

**Decision.** [`conditions.ts`](../../ab/conditions.ts) derives what is measurable from the ladder instead of typing it: the smallest video segment (300 kbps for 2 s, 75 kB) must last six times the threshold, 30 ms, which makes **20 000 kbps** the fastest link on which the estimator still hears it (the figure of ADR-095), or a first-byte delay of at least 30 ms after the jitter. The validator computes, for every condition, the stretches of a session from the first media request in which **no rule** makes downloads measurable. For a pipeline check each is a **warning**, repeated in the report; for every other kind it is an **error**: an experiment runs on **finite links only**, because a stretch the estimator cannot hear is a stretch in which the two configurations are compared on the speed of the machine. The catalog's `bandwidth-drop` has such a stretch (its first 8 seconds are unthrottled, then 600 kbps), so it cannot be registered as it is. An experiment on rebuffering registers an **explicit finite variant**, written out as rules and named in the plan (`profile: null`, an id of its own such as `bandwidth-drop-finite`): for example a throttle of 20 000 kbps from the start together with the catalog's 600 kbps from 8 seconds after the first media request (the slowest throttle wins, so the link is 600 kbps from then on): the same drop, with a link before it that the estimator can hear. `high-latency` (300 ms, jitter 100 ms) has no such stretch and is registered by name.

**B is several settings together.** Configuration B overrides `abr.useNetworkInformation` (off), `abr.defaultBandwidthEstimate` (500 kbps), `abr.switchInterval` (4 s) and `streaming.bufferingGoal` (20 s) at once (ADR-064). The experiments compare the **two configurations as shipped**; arms of A plus one setting were considered and **not built** (they would multiply the sessions by the number of settings and answer a different question: which setting, not whether B is better than A). So no sentence of a plan or of a report may attribute an effect to one setting. The validator refuses a plan whose free text (title, hypotheses, rationales, rules, the basis of a threshold) names a setting of B ([`settings.ts`](../../ab/settings.ts), from the knobs of `src/player/configs.ts`, so a fifth setting is covered the day it is added); and every report carries one sentence that lists the settings with Shaka's defaults and says that no effect found can be credited to one of them. A test scans the whole report and fails if a single setting is named anywhere but the table of the arms and that sentence.

**What every report says it cannot say.** A section of the report, generated from the plan and the data, lists the limits that apply: one engine, and that the first rung depends on the engine (Chromium starts A on 720p from the reported downlink, WebKit and Firefox on 360p then 720p, ADR-064), localhost, synthetic content and software decoding on a machine whose load is recorded; one condition, and nothing about another network; that **B is several settings together**, so an effect cannot be credited to one of them; what an interval means; that one A/A run is significant one time in twenty; and the two that bear on these experiments in particular:

- **The first rung is part of the difference.** A starts on 720p in Chromium and B on 240p everywhere, so a startup difference may be the start rung and nothing else. The report counts the start rung of every session of each arm, says when the arms never shared one, and gives an **exploratory** comparison within the rungs both arms started on (at least five sessions of each arm), labelled as not randomised, with raw p-values and no verdict.
- **When a stall starts in Chromium.** The rebuffer metric counts Shaka's `buffering` episodes. In Chromium the audio can keep the media clock running while video has a gap, so a stall is counted when the audio runs out, not when the picture freezes (the note on what a stall is in Chromium in [e2e-rec.md](e2e-rec.md)). A difference confined to video-only stalls cannot be seen.

**Alternatives considered.** Named profiles only (simple, and silently dependent on the speed of the machine). Making an unmeasured stretch a warning for experiments (the first version of this record: the warning is read once, and a condition with a deaf start would still have run, with a footnote in the report). Arms of A plus one setting (a different question, and several times the sessions). Writing the limits once in a document (they depend on the data: the start rungs of this run, the load of this machine).

**Defending it.** "The report ends with what it cannot say, and the sentences are written from the sessions, so they are true of this run. A configuration that starts on a lower rung has not necessarily started faster because of anything else, and the report says so before I do."
