# The live suites decisions (ADR-098 to ADR-100)

Architecture decision records for the six live specs of the lab, PB-LIVE-001 to PB-LIVE-006 in [`tests/e2e/live.spec.ts`](../../tests/e2e/live.spec.ts), the helpers they share in [`tests/e2e/live/`](../../tests/e2e/live) (the live edge as the origin keeps it, the readings taken at each refresh, the latency series, the `measured` annotation) and the unit tests of those helpers ([`tests/unit/e2e-live`](../../tests/unit/e2e-live)). They stand on the support library ([ADR-070 to ADR-079](e2e.md)), on the live simulation ([CONTRACTS.md](../CONTRACTS.md) section 3 and [ADR-084](server.md)) and on the definition of live latency ([ADR-066](player.md), [METRICS.md](../METRICS.md)), and they judge against the numbers of [`src/shared/criteria.ts`](../../src/shared/criteria.ts), which are initial policy ([ADR-023](strategy.md)). Each record says what the situation was, what was chosen, what was rejected, what it costs, and how I would argue for it.

**Where the numbers come from.** One machine: Apple M2 Pro (12 cores, shared with other work: the load average read between 7 and 21 during the runs, from other agents' browsers), macOS 27.2, Node 22.11.0, Playwright 1.64.1, Chromium 156.0.8078.4 and WebKit 27.2 natively, Firefox 157.0 and Linux WebKit 27.2 in the Playwright image through `npm run docker:pw -- --cpus 3` (Docker, arm64), two workers, on the evening of 2026-11-03 (UTC-6; the containers' clock already read 2026-11-04), with the specs as committed at `66688f4` on the branch `p3/live` (what was committed after it changes a comment and documents, not what a spec asserts). The distributions are from stability runs of the whole file: five runs in a row in Chromium (the CPU-throttled project runs PB-LIVE-001 in each of them) and three runs in a row in each of WebKit on macOS, Firefox and Linux WebKit, fourteen runs in all; every spec passed every one of them (ADR-099 says what the two earlier runs that did not were). Nothing was measured on a CI runner, on x64 or on a GitHub macOS runner, the stream is the lab's synthetic one, and the limits are the policy of `criteria.ts`: the specs assert behaviour against them, not these numbers.

## ADR-098: The playhead is judged by two readings, the player's latency and the origin's published edge, because the hook does not expose the seek range

**Context.** PB-LIVE-001 asks for a join "near the live edge", PB-LIVE-003 and PB-LIVE-004 for a playhead "inside the seek range". The hook ([`QAMetrics.live`](../../src/shared/qa.ts)) reports `latencyMs`, `latencyMaxMs`, `presentationDelayMs` and `edgeSeeks`, and `playback.currentTime`; it does not report Shaka's seek range, and a spec never evaluates in the page (ADR-070). `latencyMs` is Shaka's own idea of the distance to the newest published point (ADR-066): the player grading itself. The service knows what it really published: `GET /api/sessions/:id` returns the highest segment number published at that moment, and the request log timestamps every request, so "what had the origin published when this was asked" is the contract's formula, `floor((t - availabilityStartTime) / d)`. The two are not the same quantity. Shaka's edge is continuous, the origin publishes whole segments, so for a playhead that plays steadily the origin's distance is a sawtooth of one segment while the player's reading is flat: in an exploratory run in Chromium (22 reads, one per second of media time) the player read 6.15 s every time and the origin's distance ran between 4.9 and 6.0 s. And two facts of Shaka 5.3.3 bound what "inside the seek range" can mean: `Playhead.reposition_` brings a playhead that is past `seekRange().end` back only when it repositions after a seek, and `onPollWindow_` moves a playhead only when it has fallen behind the start of the range, and not at all while the element is paused (`lib/media/playhead.js`). A playhead a little beyond the end of the range during playback is therefore not an error of the player: in the first Firefox series the latency read 5,627 ms at one playlist refresh of PB-LIVE-004, 0.37 s under the 6 s presentation delay, and nothing was wrong.

**Decision.** Every live spec reads both, and asks them different questions. The player's reading is judged against the limits of `criteria.ts` (ADR-100): the p95 limit at the first frame and after the return to the edge, the maximum limit over the 20 s window, no catch-up seek, no rebuffer; and, for "inside the seek range", that the latency is positive (the playhead is behind the edge) and not more than the DVR depth taken from the session's live clock (it has not fallen out of the window). The origin's reading is judged as bounds, never as equality, and read right after the player's numbers: the playhead is not past the newest segment the origin has published, not behind it by more than the maximum limit (the p95 limit plus one segment: the granularity of that reading), and not before the start of the oldest segment of its window, `(edge - windowSegments) x d`. On the origin's side the join also reads the request log: the first video segment requested is at most the p95 limit in segments plus one behind the edge at the instant it was asked (2 to 4 segments in the runs), and the origin refused nothing (a request outside the window is a 404). A failure prints both readings: the playhead, the origin's edge and the first segment requested, which is where the catalog tells the reader to look.

**Alternatives considered.** Only the player's latency: a manifest that misstates `availabilityStartTime`, or a player with a wrong clock, agrees with itself, and the failure text of the row sends the reader to the service clock, the edge number and the first segment requested, which are facts of the origin. `seekRange()` through `page.evaluate`: specs never cross the boundary, the hook is the contract, and the handle has no such member. Asserting that the latency equals the presentation delay within a tolerance: the join latency is the delay plus the startup time (6,062 to 7,172 ms over 47 joins, ADR-100), and a tolerance narrow enough to mean anything fails on a slow runner. The p95 limit for the origin's reading: it differs from the player's by up to a segment either way (5.6 to 7.5 s against 6.2 to 7.2 s at the 19 joins of PB-LIVE-001), so it would fail a join that the player's own number passes.

**Consequences.** The check is weaker than Shaka's own seek range: it cannot tell a playhead 0.4 s beyond the end of the range from one inside it, nor see the start-of-range margin Shaka keeps, and the end of the range is not enforced during playback anyway. A hook that exposed `seekRange` in `metrics().live` would let PB-LIVE-003 assert `start <= currentTime <= end` directly: it is a library need in the report. The origin's edge is read a few milliseconds after the player's numbers, which can only make its distance larger, by at most a segment, and the maximum limit has that allowance in it. The edge formula is written once, in the helper (`edgeAt`), and a unit test pins it to the contract's example (segment 15 at 30 s on air, 14 at 29.999 s).

**Defending it.** "A player that grades its own distance to the live edge can be wrong in the same direction as its clock, and the origin is the only one that knows what it published. So every live spec reads both, holds the player to the limits of the policy and the origin to bounds, and prints both when it fails."

## ADR-099: Live time passes on the origin's clock: refreshes are counted on its log, the pause is measured in segments it published, and what is not asserted

**Context.** A live spec runs in real time (a segment every 2 s, a manifest refresh every 2 s: five refreshes are at least 10 s), and the rules forbid sleeps, timers and frame loops of a spec's own. Two things a live spec must wait for are not player events: a manifest refresh, and the live clock moving on while the viewer is paused. Some of its claims are negative (nothing happens while paused), which is what `observeFrames` is for, but that window is a number of frames, and "until the origin has published four more segments" is not.

**Decision.** (1) A refresh is an entry of the request log: kind `manifest`, answered with a 200, not pending and not abandoned (`answeredManifest`); the answer that comes with the load is the first and is not counted. After each answer the spec reads the player and the origin (a checkpoint), so every read comes after the window slid on. The observation lasts until there were five refreshes **and** the origin's edge, as of the last one, is five segments past the one the load listed. The first series showed why: a Firefox run in a cold container answered five refreshes of the audio playlist in 7.5 s (gaps of 2.4, 2.1, 2.1, 0.55 and 0.49 s), so the window had slid three segments and the playhead had played 6.9 s; the unit tests include that burst. Intervals shorter than one second are folded into the next before the media clock is judged, because over a few hundred milliseconds "it did not advance" is the granularity of the clock. (2) HLS is paced by the audio playlist. The player reloads the playlists of the variant it plays together; the audio has one representation, while the video playlist is another file after every ABR switch. In the runs the video playlist was answered 6 times in Chromium (the load and five refreshes) and 7 times in WebKit on macOS, Firefox and Linux WebKit. In WebKit on macOS the extra answer is the 720p playlist, loaded at the switch from 360p 2.2 s after the load (a scratch run of the request log: `v360`, `a-aac` at the load, `v720` after the first refresh, and only `v720` and `a-aac` from then on); Firefox and Linux WebKit show the same count. Counting video playlist requests would have counted the switch as a refresh. The video playlist is still held to the count, with a positive wait. (3) Latency over 20 s is one read per whole second of media time, taken on the media clock (`waitForMediaTime`, then `metrics()`), and a failure prints the series, including the part of it before a stall. What is judged is the player's own maximum (`latencyMaxMs`, updated at every `timeupdate`), not the twenty reads; the p95 of the reads and the drift (last minus first) are recorded and not judged, because they are the claims of PB-RDY-002 and PB-RDY-005. (4) The pause of PB-LIVE-006 lasts until the origin has answered a manifest that lists the edge it had published when the viewer paused plus four segments: a positive event of the log, counted in segments, not in seconds (the pause was 7.2 to 9.0 s of wall clock in the runs). The negative claims are asserted over the recorded timeline after that bound: no `playing`, `seeking`, `seeked`, `live-edge-seek` or `error` between the pause and the resume, and the media clock where it was (within 0.1 s). The resume is the positive control: the first `playing` is at the paused position (within 0.5 s), no seek follows, and the clock gets two seconds beyond it. No live spec uses `observeFrames`. (5) A request the player abandoned is not a refusal. PB-LIVE-001, 003, 004 and 005 assert that the origin refused nothing (no 4xx or 5xx: a segment outside the window is a 404) with `refusedRequests`, and not with the library's `status: 'error'` filter, which also counts `aborted` entries. In the first Firefox series PB-LIVE-005 failed on exactly that: after the jump back to the edge Shaka had abandoned its request for segment 15 (a 200 with no body and `aborted: true`), and the attachments of the failure (`request-log.json`, `qa-events.json`) show no refusal. (6) Not asserted, on purpose: the cadence limits of the readiness criteria (the longest and the median gap between refreshes are PB-RDY-004's; the gaps are recorded and printed in the failure, 1,931 to 2,403 ms over the fourteen runs, medians 1,999 to 2,007 ms); the start limit at the join (PB-RDY-001); the p95 of the window (PB-RDY-002); the first segments requested in HLS, because Shaka's parser reads the middle segment of the video playlist to learn its basic information and up to two more to probe for closed captions (segments 8, 11 and 4 at edge 15 in the request log of Chromium, before the playback segments; `manifest.hls.disableClosedCaptionsDetection` is off by default); and the media clock against the threshold that a media-time wait resolved on.

**A race the first series found.** `waitForMediaTime(t)` resolves on the media time of a presented frame, which can be a few milliseconds ahead of `video.currentTime` when that is read next. PB-LIVE-006 asserted `currentTime >= paused position + 2` after its wait and failed once in the first five Chromium runs (28.057777 against 28.059135, 1.4 ms short; attachments `qa-events.json`, `qa-metrics.json`, `request-log.json`, `session-detail.json`). The assertion is gone and the wait is the evidence: a player that stalled at the resume rejects it, with its timeline. Two reads of two clocks must not be compared with the threshold of one of them.

**Alternatives considered.** Counting seconds (a sleep with another name). Polling `GET /api/sessions/:id` for the edge between `observeFrames` windows (a polling loop in a spec: the log is already the polled channel, in lab code). Counting video playlist requests for HLS (above). Judging every pair of consecutive refreshes however close they are (a burst reads as a stall). Judging the cadence here (a duplicate of PB-RDY-004, which would block a merge over a readiness number).

**Consequences.** The observation of five refreshes takes at least 10 s, and 2 s more when the window has to catch up with a burst. The pause ends at the first refresh after the fourth segment, so its length varies, and the growth of the latency across it is asserted with two segments of slack (`(published - 2) x d`: 7.2 s was the smallest growth measured against a threshold of 4 s, and a resume that jumps to the edge grows it by about nothing). PB-LIVE-006 depends on the player refreshing the manifest while paused: it does (four or five manifests during the pause in every run); a player that stopped would fail it with a message that says so, which is arguably a finding of its own.

**Defending it.** "'The window slid' and 'the viewer was paused for four segments' are facts of the origin, so the specs wait for the origin to say them and only then ask the player where it is. Nothing in them counts seconds; the only time they let the page run is a wait on the media clock, which is an event."

## ADR-100: The limits are imported, a green run keeps its numbers, and a limit that reality does not contradict can still be loose

**Context.** The limits of `criteria.ts` are initial policy that Phase 7 validates: if reality contradicts one, that is a finding with the measured distribution, not a reason to change the limit or the test. A suite that only says "green" cannot give that distribution when it is wanted: it would have to be reconstructed from flake reports.

**Decision.** The limits are imported, never copied: `LIVE_LIMITS.latencyP95MaxMs` (8 s) for the first frame (PB-LIVE-001) and the return to the edge (PB-LIVE-005), `LIVE_LIMITS.latencyMaxMs` (10 s) for the 20 s window (PB-LIVE-002) and for the origin's reading (ADR-098). The segment, the DVR depth and the presentation delay come from the session's live clock, never from a literal. The tolerances that belong to the specs are constants with a reason, each one measured against the runs:

| Constant | Value | What it separates | Measured |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `MAX_EDGE_SEEKS` | 0 | Shaka's live-sync is off in configurations A and B, so a catch-up jump is the playhead having fallen behind: the first one is the finding | 0 in 14 windows of 20 s |
| `LANDING_TOLERANCE_SEC` | 1 s | a rewind that lands where it was asked from one clipped to the start of the window | 0.000 to 0.270 s; 5.96 s in the mutation |
| `RESUME_TOLERANCE_SEC`, `HELD_TOLERANCE_SEC` | 0.5 s, 0.1 s | a resume from the paused position from a jump; a paused clock from a moving one | 0.000 to 0.144 s; 0.000 s |
| `MIN_ADVANCE_SHARE` | 0.5 | a player that plays on between refreshes from one that stalled | the clock advanced the whole interval; 0.00 to 0.16 s of 2.00 s in the mutation |
| growth of the latency across the pause | `(published - 2) x d`, 4 s | a resume from the paused position from a jump towards the edge | 7.2 to 9.0 s |

Over the 47 joins of the runs (PB-LIVE-001 and the joins that PB-LIVE-005 and PB-LIVE-006 start with) the player's latency at the first frame was 6,062 to 7,172 ms, median 6,224, against 8,000; over the fourteen windows the maximum was 6,201 to 6,960 ms against 10,000, with no catch-up seek and no rebuffer. That is for Phase 7 to tighten from distributions like these.

**Alternatives considered.** Copying the numbers into the specs (they would drift from the policy, and Phase 7 changes them in one place). Asserting the measured distribution (a limit of 6.5 s would fail Linux WebKit's joins and mean nothing the policy says). Keeping the numbers only in a document written by hand (it would not be reproducible: the annotations are). Skipping a spec in the engine where a limit is tight (a silent skip; the gap list is the only place for that).

**Consequences.** A green run is evidence with numbers, and a report of many runs is a distribution. The annotations are one more JSON in the report, a few hundred bytes. The tolerances are the specs' own judgments and are written next to the evidence that places them; a change of the player that moves one of them has to move the number with its reason. Findings that are not about a spec and are in the report: the HLS join downloads three video segments from the middle of the window before it plays (ADR-099), the hook has no seek range (ADR-098), and a media-time wait and `metrics()` are two clocks.

**Defending it.** "The limits are the policy's, so the specs read them from where the policy lives; the numbers are the runs', so every run keeps them. When the two disagree, the lab says which, with a distribution: today they do not disagree, and the interesting thing the distribution says is how far from the limits the player actually is."
