# The adaptive-bitrate specs decisions (ADR-095 to ADR-097, and ADR-106)

Architecture decision records for the seven ABR specs of the lab, [`tests/e2e/abr.spec.ts`](../../tests/e2e/abr.spec.ts) (PB-ABR-001 to PB-ABR-007), and the helpers they stand on, [`tests/e2e/abr/`](../../tests/e2e/abr) (the ladder and what a link carries, the windows derived from Shaka's switch interval, Shaka's bans and the bound of the first step up, the invalid run, the bound on the first frame, the comparison of the player's history with the origin's log, the sentence in front of a failed wait), with their unit tests in [`tests/unit/e2e-abr`](../../tests/unit/e2e-abr). They build on ADR-005 (events with a cursor), ADR-064 (configuration A is Shaka's defaults, and its first rung depends on the engine), ADR-070 to ADR-079 in [e2e.md](e2e.md) (the support library, the documented gaps, the diagnostics) and ADR-052 in [server-delivery.md](server-delivery.md) (the throttle). ## ADR-095: A condition of an ABR test is a finite link derived from the ladder, and a recovery replaces the throttle instead of removing it

**Context.** The catalog names the conditions in words: "an unconstrained network" (PB-ABR-001), "the throttle is removed once the downshift has happened" (PB-ABR-003). On the lab's origin with no rule a segment of 75 kB arrives in a fraction of a millisecond (the log's `totalMs` for the 240p segments after the rule was removed: 0.1 to 1.3 ms). Shaka's `SimpleAbrManager.segmentDownloaded` ignores a download that took less than `abr.cacheLoadThreshold` (5 ms by default) and treats it as a cache hit: no sample reaches the estimator. Whether an engine's estimate hears of a fast network therefore depends on how slow that engine's fetch path is, which is a property of the machine and the engine, not of the player's adaptation. Measured on this machine, with the first literal reading of PB-ABR-003 (`bandwidth-drop`, the rule removed right after the step down): **Chromium stayed on 240p for the 28.6 s it was watched** (media time 16.4 s to 45.0 s: no `variant-changed` at all; in a mutation run of the spec, below, the title played to its end on 240p); with `abr.cacheLoadThreshold: 0` the same Chromium stepped up 9.6 s after the removal (360p, then 720p 8 s later); **macOS WebKit stepped up** on the plain removal, 9.6 s after it to 360p and 27.3 s after it to 720p. One condition, two verdicts, and the player is the same.

**Decision.** (1) "Unconstrained" is a throttle of **20,000 kbps** (10.8 times the top variant's declared 1,845 kbps): finite, so that the 164 kB segment of 360p takes 65 ms and every download is long enough to be measured, and far above anything the ladder needs. (2) A recovery **replaces** the throttle rule by one at **6000 kbps** (3.2 times the top variant), in one `setFaults` call, so the lowest rung's 75 kB segment takes 100 ms. (3) What a link "carries" is derived from the catalog's declared bandwidths (`bestRungCarriedBy(ladder, kbps)`), never typed: PB-ABR-002 settles on the best rung of a 600 kbps link, PB-ABR-006 asserts that this is the lowest rung, PB-ABR-005 asserts that the pinned rung's declared bandwidth is more than its 1500 kbps link, PB-ABR-003 and PB-ABR-001 assert that their links carry the whole ladder. A change of the ladder fails there, with the numbers, instead of making a test pass or fail for the wrong reason.

**Alternatives considered.** Remove the rule as the catalog says: it is red in Chromium and green in WebKit for a reason that belongs to the lab's network model (the finding is reported, ADR-097 and the report). Put `abr.cacheLoadThreshold: 0` in the test (it changes the player under test: configuration A is Shaka's defaults, ADR-064). Use `clean` for PB-ABR-001 (on that profile the climb of WebKit and Firefox depends on their fetch taking more than 5 ms; it climbed in every run here, and nothing guarantees it on a faster machine). Slow the origin by a latency rule (it delays the first byte, which the estimator counts as download time only for the first byte of a response; it does not make the transfer measurable).

**Consequences.** Two numbers in the spec file, each with its arithmetic. A deviation from the catalog's wording, reported as a correction. A 20 Mbps link is not "no link": PB-ABR-002 and PB-ABR-007 start on the profile `bandwidth-drop`, whose first 8 s are unthrottled, and they are written so that it does not matter (ADR-096). On the recovered link the engines agree, which they did not on the removed rule: from the recovery to the first step up, Chromium 8.0 to 9.9 s, WebKit 8.0 to 9.6 s, Firefox 8.0 to 8.1 s, Linux WebKit 7.8 to 9.9 s on the author's machine; the bound of the spec is 14 s where no ban can have held a rung and 33 s where one can (the amendment below).

**Defending it.** "The catalog says what the member experiences; the test has to say it with a network the player can measure. A network that answers in a tenth of a millisecond is not a better network, it is one that Shaka is built to ignore, and I can show the two engines disagreeing about it."

**Amendment: the first CI evidence, a second bound, and why.** *What failed.* In the nightly run 41793007785 (GitHub macOS runner, WebKit 27.2, 3 CPUs, one worker, job 126450559420) PB-ABR-003 failed both attempts: the first step up came 21.9 s and 16.4 s of media time after the recovery, against the bound of 14 s (7.8 to 9.9 s on the author's machine, in four engines). On the same run Chromium, Firefox and Linux WebKit passed it at 8.2, 9.8 and 8.2 s, and in the nightly 41785550198 (two workers) they passed it at 9.8, 9.5 and 9.8 s and the macOS runner at 7.8 s. The attachments of the failed attempts rebuild the timeline ([`docs/results/e2e-abr/gha-macos-arm64-upshift.json`](../results/e2e-abr/gha-macos-arm64-upshift.json)); the page clock below starts at `load-requested`:

| --- | --- | --- |
| rung changes | 360p start; 720p at 0.73 s; **360p at 12.31 s**; **240p at 20.31 s**; **720p at 42.40 s** (media 42.00 s) | 360p start; 720p at 1.11 s; 360p at 17.40 s; **240p at 22.32 s**; **720p at 39.95 s** (media 38.35 s) |

| frames dropped | 37 of 1271 (2.9 %) | 40 of 1164 (3.4 %) |

*What it shows.* (1) **The condition was the one the ADR describes**: the origin delivered the recovered link at 7.3 Mbps, 3.8 and 9.3 times the thresholds of the two rungs above 240p (the replay of Shaka's estimator over the origin's own durations, `fast` half-life 2 s, `slow` 5 s, weights by download time, puts the estimate over 360p's 786 kbps at 21.6 s in attempt 0 and 23.8 s in attempt 1, and over the top rung's 1,941 kbps at 38.6 s in attempt 0 and, with 20 ms of fetch overhead per sample, at 40.0 s in attempt 1: without the overhead it is 1,790 kbps then). The page was the slow part: it dropped frames, and Chromium, Firefox and Linux WebKit of the same run dropped none (0 of 359 to 365). (2) **Two of the changes are not decisions of the adaptation.** It decides at most once per `abr.switchInterval` (8 s) and every choice, a ban's included, restarts the wait. In attempt 0 the first choice was at 0.73 s (the start-up shortcut) and the next at the first download after 8.73 s (an audio segment, at 8.82 s), so the next evaluation could not come before 16.8 s: the change at 12.31 s is out of its clock. In attempt 1 the change to 240p came 4.9 s after the one to 360p, and in the same run PB-ABR-002 stepped down 5.2 s after its first step down (media time). On the engines that drop no frame, the two step downs of PB-ABR-002 were 8.04, 8.09 and 8.05 s apart. (3) **The step ups line up with the ends of bans.** In 5.3.3 the only choices that skip the interval are `Player.disableStream`, which the dropped-frame protection calls (`abr.droppedFrames`, on by default in configuration A: 15 % of the frames of a 2 s window dropped disables every variant of the playing video stream for 30 s and chooses again at once) and which a failing request calls (every response of the log is a 200), and the timer that gives the variants back every second (`Player.checkVariants_`) and chooses again at once. Attempt 0 fits two bans, of 720p at 12.31 s and of 360p at 20.31 s: the estimate allowed 360p from 21.6 s on and the player stayed on 240p for 22 s; at 42.40 s, 30.09 s after the first ban began (30 ticks of the 1 s timer), the first ban ended and the player chose 720p, skipping the rung whose ban lasted to 50.3 s. Attempt 1 fits one ban, of 360p at 22.32 s: with 360p unavailable the player needs the estimate of the top rung (1,941 kbps, where 786 is enough for 360p), and the replay crosses it at the 25th segment, 40.0 s, where the step up was at 39.95 s. (4) **Why the estimate is slow after a recovery anyway:** its clock is the time spent downloading, not the time that passes. A 75 kB segment at 7 Mbps is 85 ms of weight and comes once in 2 s, so the slow average, with its half-life of 5 s, moves by 1.2 % of the distance to the new rate per segment: about three segments to cross 786 kbps (the 14 s of the bound), about twenty to cross 1,941 kbps (the case of a banned 360p).

*Decision.* It is the player's behaviour on a machine that drops frames, not a fault of the origin or of the condition, and the oracle has to know it. The bound of the first step up has **two values, chosen by what the run itself shows** (`upshiftBound` in [`tests/e2e/abr/bans.ts`](../../tests/e2e/abr/bans.ts), with unit tests on synthetic timelines of the runs above): where no frame was dropped and no change came sooner than the interval allows, no ban can have been in force, and the bound is the link's, **14 s**; where either happened, a ban may have held the rungs above, and the bound is the ban's: **33 s** (its 30 s, the 1 s tick that gives the variants back, and one segment of margin; a ban that was in force at the recovery began no later than it, so no ban outlasts it by more, whatever the order of the bans, and by then the estimate has had 30 s of the recovered link). The two readings need no browser name and no list of environments: they are the page's own counters and its own timeline, read when the step up is found, and the annotation of the result says which bound applied and why (`bound 33 s, a ban may have held a rung; dropped frames 37 of 1271`). Any dropped frame counts, and not a larger number, because a ban needs 15 % of the frames of one window and the window after a rung change or a stall holds only the few frames rendered since (the manager restarts its count at every switch it makes). A player that never climbs fails in both (the wait is 43 s, then the message names the dropped frames and the forced changes); a player that climbs after 34 s or after a minute fails in both; a player that needs 20 s on a machine that dropped nothing fails against 14 s. The messages of PB-ABR-001, -002 and -006 now say, with the dropped frames, which changes came sooner than the interval allows (`banNote`).

*Alternatives considered.* Widening the single bound to 33 s (it would hide a regression from 10 to 30 s in the three engines that drop nothing, which is where it is cheap to see). Setting `abr.droppedFrames: false` in this spec (it changes the player under test, ADR-064, and it removes the finding: that after a recovery the protection can hold the player on the lowest rung for 30 s). Skipping the test when frames were dropped (a skip is silence, and on this runner it would be every run). Keying the bound on `envId` or on the engine (it would be 33 s on a macOS runner that, one day, drops nothing). Retrying (the flake gate retries once, and both attempts failed).

*Costs and what is not proved.* On a run that dropped frames, the test cannot tell a ban from a slow adaptation of up to 33 s; the three engines that drop nothing still hold the 14 s, and the adaptation is the same code in all of them. The explanation is the only one that fits every number above, and it is not proved: the hook records a change by Shaka's `adaptation` event (a decision) and by `variantchanged` (a ban, its end, a failover) alike, as `abr`, and the page counters have no time axis. A hook that recorded which event announced the change, or when the frames were dropped, would turn the inference into a reading (reported as a library need).

**Amendment 2: skipping, now that the evidence is the shape of a timeline and not a count (ADR-106).** The alternative "skipping the test when frames were dropped" was rejected above: a rule on the count alone would have skipped both attempts of PB-ABR-003 (37 and 40 frames dropped) whatever their timeline. What changed is the question and the evidence. PB-ABR-003 waits for a climb, and a bound can absorb a late one; PB-ABR-002 and PB-ABR-006 assert that *nothing changes* after the step down, and no bound absorbs a climb that should not have happened. The flake tracker then found 6 of 60 first attempts of PB-ABR-002 on the same runner (10 %) failing on that assertion, and the four of them that kept a trace were one shape: a climb to 360p, a rung the 600 kbps link cannot carry, 4.0 to 6.0 s after the step down, with frames dropped and no failed request. ADR-106 skips only an attempt that shows that shape, says so loudly with the numbers, and counts it; an attempt that does not show it fails as before. It is narrower than "any dropped frame" on four counts (the first change after the step down, above the rung the link carries, inside the switch interval, with no failed request) and it is not silent: an `invalid-run` annotation and attachment, and its reason in the CI summary and in the flake report. PB-ABR-003 keeps the bound of this ADR, because a climb is what it waits for.

## ADR-096: The oracle is an event or a request, never the clock; the windows are derived from Shaka's interval, and the start rung is nobody's business but the engine's

**Context.** Adaptation runs on seconds: Shaka decides at most every `abr.switchInterval` (8 s, configuration A has no override), when a segment download completes or makes progress, from the minimum of two moving averages weighted by download time. Its first rung is the engine's (Chromium 720p from `navigator.connection.downlink`, WebKit and Firefox 360p), and what it does next is not one pattern: under `bandwidth-drop` the step to 240p is one decision in 8 of 10 Chromium runs (at 16.2 to 16.5 s in 6, 8 s after the evaluation that preceded the drop, and at 10.0 s in 2: Shaka's start-up shortcut lets the first decision through at once when its estimate becomes good only after the link has weakened) and two decisions (720p to 360p to 240p, 8 s apart) in the other 2; two decisions in 10 of 14 WebKit runs, and in every one of the 6 Firefox and 6 Linux WebKit runs. A test that sleeps, or that expects the sequence 720p, 240p, fails on the machine that was not the author's.

**Decision.** (1) Every wait is an event with a cursor (`variant-changed` with a `detail` filter, or the first request of a rung in the log) with a generous timeout that is a bound on a failure and never the pace of a test, derived where it can be (the step down: 8 s until the link weakens + two decisions 8 s apart + one 720p segment in flight at 600 kbps, 6 s, + margin = 45 s). (2) Where the claim is that nothing happens, a window bounded by a positive event closes it, in media time, derived from the interval: **the interval plus two segments (12 s)** (`settleWindowSeconds`), because nothing can change before the interval has elapsed and the manager decides on a segment download; a manager that flaps shows inside it. The first step up is bounded by the interval plus three segments (14 s; measured 8.0 to 9.8 s) where no ban can have held a rung, and by the ban's 30 s, the 1 s tick and one segment (33 s) where one can (the amendment of ADR-095). (3) The start rung is never asserted. PB-ABR-001 waits for "the top rung" however the player got there (Chromium starts on it: the claim that counts there is "stays"), PB-ABR-002 for the rung the link carries, PB-ABR-004 chooses a target different from the current rung, PB-ABR-005 pins after a few seconds of playback so that the pin is the first thing that moves the rung, and PB-ABR-006 derives its bound for the rung it started on. (4) Every claim about a rung is made on both channels: the player's events and, independently, the origin's log: "from the first request of the rung on, no other video segment was asked for" and "the last three segments delivered are of that rung". The line is drawn at the first request of the new rung, not at a time, because the request that is in flight when the player switches may be of the old rung. (5) A pass that consists of "nothing changed" is protected against vacuity: PB-ABR-005 requires the origin to show a pinned-rung segment that took longer than its 2 s of playing time, so that it cannot pass on a lab that applied no throttle. (6) PB-ABR-006's first frame is bounded by what the link must carry before it, **at most 251 kB** on a start that is too heavy for the link (128 kB that Shaka must sample before it trusts its estimate, two 16 kB chunks of the throttle's grants, the lowest rung's first segment, the audio's, the inits) at 600 kbps less the throttle's 10 % tolerance, **3.7 s, plus 2 s** for everything that is not the link (six times the slowest unthrottled first frame of the smoke runs): 5.7 s, against 2.8 to 3.7 s measured in four engines. (7) PB-ABR-004 plays through the first segment of the new rung, because Shaka keeps what is buffered and applies the choice to what is fetched next: the new resolution reaches the playhead about 10 s after the click, and that is where a broken change breaks.

**Alternatives considered.** Asserting a rung sequence or a switch count (wrong, above). `expect.poll` or fixed windows in seconds (the interval is Shaka's wall clock, the window must be media time, and both must survive a loaded machine). A predicate such as "any rung above 240p" in the wait (the page boundary cannot carry a function, ADR-070: the first change after the cursor is read instead, and the spec checks that it is a step up). A tighter bound on the first frame (the bound is thin by design for one regression: with the player made to trust its estimate only after 1 GB, which means it fetches the whole 720p segment, the first frame is at 5.87 s against the bound of 5.72 s, so the margin that catches that regression is 0.15 s).

**Consequences.** The tests are slow and say "it ended on 240p", not "how fast it got there": speed is the performance gate's (PB-PERF-003). The wide bounds would not catch a step down that takes 40 s where it takes 17. A mutation that did not turn PB-ABR-002 red: `abr.switchInterval: 60`, because in Chromium Shaka's first good estimate arrives after the link weakened and its start-up shortcut (`lastTimeChosenMs -= switchInterval`) then allows the decision at once. The ABR tests record the dropped frames of the load in the message of a rung change (`banNote`), and, when the timeline proves it, the changes that came sooner than the interval allows: Shaka also leaves a rung when 15 % of the frames of a 2 s window are dropped, whatever the link, and then it does not wait. A failed wait gets the same note from the page (`orExplain` takes an addendum, called only when the wait failed).

**Defending it.** "The player's adaptation has a clock of its own. I wait for what it says it did and I check what the origin saw it do; the windows are the interval it is bound by, so when a window is too short the arithmetic says why, not a feeling about this machine."

## ADR-097: PB-ABR-007 compares two accounts as a story, and says what is left unresolved

**Context.** The hook is ours, and its history feeds the baselines and the experiments (`bitrate.switches`, `bitrate.history`). The origin's log is the independent account of which rung was fetched. Comparing two numbers (a count) agrees by accident: the history `720p, 240p` and a log of `720p, 360p, 240p` have different counts, but `720p, 360p` and `720p, 240p` have the same one.

**Decision.** `compareHistoryWithLog` (a pure function with 12 unit tests) compares the **rung story**. The video segment requests of the log become runs of one rung each, in arrival order. The history entries are matched to the runs as a subsequence: the origin may not have seen a rung the player never named, or in another order, and the player may name a rung the origin never saw a segment of only if it left it within one segment duration (Firefox starts on 360p and is on 720p 12 ms later; no segment of the first can have been asked for) and never the rung it ends on. For each change the player made itself (`abr` or `manual`; the initial selection is exempt, because the hook records it once the load has resolved, a moment after the first requests) the first request of the new rung must not be earlier than the player's timestamp, and at most one segment after it. The two clocks are aligned by the one event both saw, the manifest request: the page's `load-requested` came before it and the origin received it before the page's `manifest-loaded`, so the alignment is uncertain by `metrics.startup.manifestMs` (the spec reads it from the page: a few ms on a quiet machine), plus 25 ms for the resolution of the clocks. "One segment": Shaka cuts the request in flight when the new segment is the smaller (`shouldAbortCurrentRequest_`) and otherwise lets it finish, which is under one segment of the new rung (1.2 s at 600 kbps); with a full buffer the next fetch waits at most one segment of playback. `metrics.bitrate.switches` must equal the length of the history less one.

**Measured.** The first request of a new rung comes −118 to +509 ms from the player's timestamp aligned by the manifest (Chromium −1 to +12 ms, WebKit −6 to +509, Firefox −118 to +41, Linux WebKit −40 to +93): the negative part is the uncertainty of the alignment, the positive part the time the page takes to issue the fetch, and the +509 ms is WebKit's climb to 720p half a second into the playback, when the buffer was already full of 360p and the next fetch waited for the playhead (the full-buffer case of the one-segment bound). The comparison was made to fail by stamping the history 3 s late: "the origin was asked for v240 at 16919 ms, 2951 ms before the player says it switched to 240p (abr, not before 19870 ms of the origin's clock): the player's timestamp is late".

**Alternatives considered.** Comparing counts (above). Comparing the time of the switch without the alignment (the two clocks have different origins). Aligning the clocks with `performance.timeOrigin` of the page (a spec does not evaluate code in the page, ADR-070; and the container's and the host's clocks are the same machine's, but only the manifest anchor needs no assumption). A one-segment tolerance at the start (the start is exempt for a reason, not forgiven).

**Consequences and what is not proved.** A hook that is wrong in the way the origin's log is wrong cannot be found by comparing them (the log is the service's, tested by PB-LAB-001). Nothing here says which rung was *shown*: the hook says selected, the origin says fetched, and the decoder's picture is not observed (ADR-069). The `reason` of a change cannot be checked against the origin.

**Unresolved, with the evidence.** (a) PB-ABR-006 failed once in 16 runs in Firefox in the image (3 + 10 + 3), on the loaded machine: 2.2 s after the step down to 240p, and well inside the 8 s interval, the player moved *up* to 360p, a rung a 600 kbps link cannot carry, and rebuffered three times. 13 of 366 decoded frames were dropped in the load (the hook does not say when); Shaka's dropped-frame protection (`abr.droppedFrames`, default on) disables the playing variant for 30 s when 15 % of a 2 s window is dropped, is not held back by the switch interval and leaves 360p and 720p to choose from. That fits, it was not reproduced, and it is a finding about configuration A on slow links and weak machines, not about the test: [`docs/results/e2e-abr/firefox-abr-006-flake.json`](../results/e2e-abr/firefox-abr-006-flake.json). The spec now reports the rung change before the rebuffers and names the dropped frames. **The CI gave no new evidence of it**: PB-ABR-006 passed in all eight executions of the two nightly runs (first frame at 2,898 to 3,003 ms against its bound of 5,724 ms in the four engines, no frame dropped in 359 to 366, the macOS runner's included), so Firefox stands at one failure in 18 runs. The mechanism is the one of the amendment of ADR-095, which the macOS runner showed to be real in this player (a ban of the playing 240p is the only way to leave it for 360p inside the interval), and the message of the spec now carries the proof when the timeline has it (`240p ... 2.2 s after the change before it`), so the next occurrence confirms or refutes it. The bound is not at its limit: no change.

(b) PB-ABR-002 in WebKit on macOS rebuffered once in 4 of 14 runs on the author's machine (never twice): in the four runs where the first decision came at 16.7 s or later and went through 360p (which the link does not carry) for 8 s. **On the GitHub macOS runner it rebuffered twice in one attempt of the nightly 41785550198 (32 of 1067 frames dropped), and the assertion, at most one, failed**; its retry rebuffered never, and in the nightly 41793007785 it rebuffered once. PB-ABR-003, which has the same first 24 s, rebuffered once in one of its two attempts and never in the other. The rebuffers follow the time of the first step down: a 360p segment takes 2.3 to 2.6 s to arrive at 600 kbps for 2 s of media, so after the step to 360p the buffer shrinks with every segment until the next decision, 8 s later, and the earlier the player leaves 720p the more buffer it has when it arrives. In media time: Chromium, Firefox and Linux WebKit of the runner, which drop no frame, stepped down at 16.1 to 16.3 s (no rebuffer in 6 runs), the runner's WebKit at 16.65 s (one), 16.99 s (one, PB-ABR-003) and 17.93 s (two), and the two runs in which a ban took it off 720p at 11.9 s, none. The bound of the catalog, at most one, is a product limit and is not changed. It holds, in these runs, when the first decision comes within about 17 s of media time, a number that comes from the phase between the link's 8 s and the manager's own 8 s clock (as (c) says of Chromium), that is from the profile and not from the player; a runner that decides later will fail it, and the failure is true (the member stalls twice). The message now lists the rebuffers and, with them, the dropped frames and the changes that came sooner than the interval allows. **The flake tracker's 60 repetitions since** (20 of the whole `@merge` tier, then 40 of this test alone, on the macOS runner; none in Linux Chromium, Firefox or WebKit in 30 repetitions of the tier each): 7 first attempts failed (11.7 %), and 6 of them failed on the assertion that nothing changes after the step down, four of them with a trace that shows a forced climb (ADR-106); the seventh is the cliff, two rebuffers (run 11 of the first collection; no trace was kept, but it passed the window assertion, which comes first, so nothing changed after its step down: it is not a forced climb), 1 in 60, under the trigger of 5 %. That failure stays a failure.

(c) Chromium on `bandwidth-drop` makes its first decision about 16.5 s into the playback with about 3.4 s of media buffered (read from the log: segment 10 of 2 s each ends at 20 s), and the 240p segment it then needs takes 1.1 s at 600 kbps: by that arithmetic a decision later than about 18.9 s would stall it. It came at 16.2 to 16.9 s in every run seen (16.34 s on the CI runner, no rebuffer), so the margin of about 2 s is real, and it comes from the phase between the link's 8 s and Shaka's own 8 s grid: a coincidence of the profile, not a property of the player.

**Defending it.** "Two instruments that agree to a few milliseconds on when it happened and in what order are a better argument for the instrument than any unit test of it. The test says what it does not know: it cannot say which rung was on the screen."

## ADR-106: A climb the adaptation cannot have decided, on a load that dropped frames, makes the attempt an invalid run: it is skipped, with its numbers, and counted

**Context.** The flake tracker collected the `@merge` tier on the GitHub macOS runner (macos-15, 3 vCPU, Apple M1 virtual, Playwright 1.64.1's WebKit, one worker, `flake-collect`, 20 repetitions): PB-ABR-002 failed its first attempt in 3 of 20 (runs 4 and 14 on both attempts, run 11 on the first only), while the other 14 `@merge` tests passed 20 of 20, PB-ENV-001 on the same engine among them. Then 40 repetitions of PB-ABR-002 alone, with the traces kept: 4 first-attempt failures, all four passed on the retry. That is 7 first-attempt failures in 60 (11.7 %) and 2 hard failures; the Linux Chromium, Firefox and WebKit legs, 30 repetitions of the tier each: none. The flake gate triggers at 5 % per test, so this turns the required `flake` check red once the history holds 20 executions of the macOS runner. Six of the seven failed on the same assertion, that no rung changes in the 12 s after the step down to 240p; the seventh (run 11 of the first collection) is the cliff of ADR-097 (b), two rebuffers. Only the four failures of the second collection kept a trace (runs 6, 13, 20 and 34); runs 4 and 14 of the first kept only the error signature, which names the assertion and not the change, so "this shape" below is shown for four of the six and assumed for the other two. The assertion message of each says `rung changes in the 12 s after the step down to 240p (qa.events()): none. Received "360p abr at <t> s"`. Every response was a 200; each log also holds three requests that the player cancelled at its own switches (`client-closed`: the in-flight segment of the rung it left), which are not failures. The classifier of this ADR, run on the real attachments of these four attempts (and not only on the fixtures of its unit tests, which carry the same numbers: [`tests/unit/e2e-abr/fixtures.ts`](../../tests/unit/e2e-abr/fixtures.ts); the traces themselves hold media segments and are not committed), finds each of them.

The climb is inside the 8 s that Shaka's adaptation waits between decisions in all four, with frames dropped and no failed request. That is the mechanism of ADR-095's amendment: the dropped-frame protection (it looks every 2 s, and at 15 % of the frames of the window dropped it bans the rung that plays for 30 s and chooses again at once) leaves, when the rung that plays is the lowest, only the two rungs the link cannot carry. The first downshift of each run (to 360p at media 9.9 to 11.9 s) also comes before any decision the 8 s chain allows (the first that can see the weak link comes at 16 s of media or later, ADR-097 (b)), so it fits a ban as well. On 360p each 2 s of video takes 2.3 to 2.6 s of link (ADR-097 (b)), so after the climb the buffer shrinks until the ban ends, 30 s later; the four traces end at the window assertion, before any rebuffer.

*The hypothesis, checked with the traces:* the burst of dropped frames comes when the playhead crosses into the first segment of the new, smaller rung, a few seconds after the step-down decision (2.1 to 4.1 s of media later, with the old rung's buffer still ahead). **Result: confirmed as to timing, not observed as to cause.** The time from the decision to the climb varies (4.0 to 6.0 s) and the time from the playhead's entry into the first 240p segment to the climb does not (1.89 to 1.92 s, four of four): the climb is at the first poll of the protection after the entry, and the one or two polls between the decision and the entry did nothing. The first downshift of each run, which fits a ban as well (above), sits at the same distance, 1.89 to 1.91 s, after the playhead's entry into the first 720p segment, and these eight changes fall at 0.34 to 0.39 s modulo 2 s of the page clock; the 2 s cadence of the segment downloads has the same period as the poll, so the phase does not tell the two apart. **Not shown:** the burst itself (the page counters have no time axis); what would close the arithmetic (a 2 s window holds about 60 frames and 15 % of it is 9, so if the first downshift and the climb of run 20 were both bans they needed about 18 dropped frames, and its whole load dropped 10: the window Shaka measures is shorter than the poll, as after a `resize` of the video or a switch, where it restarts its count, or the counters restart, and the traces cannot say which); and the rate per entry, because the traces are failing attempts, selected on the climb, and cannot say how often an entry is followed by a ban (per attempt, 6 of 60 failed on this assertion).

**Decision.** (1) **The classifier** (`forcedClimb` in [`tests/e2e/abr/bans.ts`](../../tests/e2e/abr/bans.ts), pure, with unit tests on the four real timelines and the cases that must still fail): the first change after the step down (what contradicts the claim first decides, so a real flap that comes first is never hidden by a forced change after it) is a *forced climb* when all of: the player made it itself, after a change of its own (`abr` after `abr`); it is to a rung above the best one the link carries; it came sooner than the 8 s switch interval after the step down (less 0.25 s for the clocks), because the adaptation decides at most once per interval, so a change sooner than that was not its decision; the load dropped at least one frame (the protection has nothing to act on without); and the origin failed no request (the only other path in Shaka 5.3.3 that skips the interval is the disabling of a rung after a failed request). Every condition is held by tests that fail without it (mutations of the final code: no interval condition, 4 tests red; no dropped-frames condition, 3; the rung condition flipped, 23, the four real runs among them; the change's own `abr` not required, 1; the previous change's, 2; no failed-request condition, 2; the last change after the step down deciding instead of the first, 2). (2) **An attempt that shows one is an invalid run**: `skipIfForcedClimb` ([`tests/e2e/abr/invalid-run.ts`](../../tests/e2e/abr/invalid-run.ts)) annotates the result `invalid-run`, attaches `invalid-run.json` (the climb, the step down, the frames, the link, every change, and how long after the playhead entered the first segment of the rung the climb came, which is the number that tests the hypothesis on every later invalid run), and calls `testInfo.skip(true, reason)`. The reason starts with the numbers, because the flake report keeps 90 characters of a cell (`invalid run: forced climb to 360p 5.9 s after the step down, 10 of 974 frames dropped. The 8 s switch interval allows no decision of the adaptation that soon ...`), and says "fits", not "was", of the protection, because the cause is not observed. It is never a pass and never silent. (3) **Where**: PB-ABR-002, before its window assertion, so the one decision covers the assertions that follow (the rung at the end, the rebuffers, the origin's account: a forced climb is what they would fail on); PB-ABR-006, before its settle assertion (the same assertion, plus at most one rebuffer in the first 10 s; the one Firefox failure it ever had was this shape, a climb 2.2 s after the step down; its first-frame check comes before and is untouched). **Not** PB-ABR-001 in this decision, though it has the same kind of assertion (no change in the 12 s after reaching the top rung): the change the same mechanism would make there is a downshift, 9.2 to 11.6 s after the climb to 720p in the four traces above (the first downshift of each run), which is neither above a rung the link carries nor inside the 8 s interval, so nothing in a timeline proves it forced; on an unconstrained link it could as well be the estimator of a starved page. It needed its own evidence, and being `@release` it had never been in a collection: it was collected later, and amendment 3 below is what that showed and what was decided. **Not** PB-ABR-003 (a climb is what it waits for, its bound already allows for bans, ADR-095, and it has no assertion on rebuffers), **not** PB-ABR-004 and PB-ABR-005 (`selectVariantByHeight` turns ABR off, and with ABR off the protection never runs), **not** PB-ABR-007 (a forced change is a real change in both accounts, which is what it compares). (4) **Counted**: the CI summary lists every skip of a job with its reason in full; the flake report's "Skipped" section now says in how many of the test's runs it was skipped; the collector prints the skipped tests of every repetition with the start of their reasons and their number in its last line, so the 40 repetitions that prove this say how many were invalid.

**Alternatives considered.** *Skip when frames were dropped*: rejected in ADR-095 and still: it would skip nearly every attempt on the macOS runner, a test that evaluates nothing. The classifier is narrower on four counts (a climb, above the carried rung, inside the interval, with no failed request), and it is loud. *Retry inside the test until an attempt is valid*: the flake gate already retries once, which is the flake it counts; 2 of the 6 retries failed on the same assertion again (too few to say whether attempts are independent, enough to say that a retry does not clear it), and an attempt takes 34 s at the median (26 to 43 s in the 67 attempts of the two collections). *Judge only the part of the attempt before the climb*: a pass on a shortened claim (4 to 6 s of the 12 s), counted as a pass in a history that means a full one; and the assertions that follow (the rung at the end, the origin's account, the rebuffers) cannot be shortened. *Ignore the forced change in the window assertion*: the same, and the rest of the attempt then fails for the same cause with other messages. *Quarantine the test on the macOS runner*: it takes the test out of the blocking run and of the suite rate for at most 7 days (docs/FLAKE.md), with the real regressions it would catch there. *Document macOS WebKit as a gap*: a gap says that an engine and OS pair cannot play the flavour; this one plays it (the other 14 `@merge` tests passed 20 of 20 on the runner, PB-ENV-001 among them). *Change configuration A* (`abr.droppedFrames: false`): ADR-064 says no, and it removes the finding that the protection can push a player off the lowest rung onto rungs the link cannot carry for 30 s, which is worth reporting upstream and not hiding. *Remove the cause in the test* (candidates: tracing off, a device scale factor of 1, a player page without `controls`): each is a guess about the load that the test itself adds on a 3 vCPU machine, no run has tested any of them (this change ran no browser), and none is applied; the collections of "To prove it" are where they would be measured.

**What this can hide.** (a) A real regression that flaps within the switch interval after the step down, only on a machine that also drops frames, is skipped there and not failed. The Linux legs (Chromium, Firefox and WebKit) dropped no frame in the nightlies and run the same adaptation code, so there it still fails; the macOS leg reads "skipped", which is the price. (b) A runner on which most attempts are invalid evaluates nothing, and a green there would mean nothing: the invalid runs are counted (CI summary, flake report, collector) and no rule bounds their share yet. 6 of 60 first attempts failed that way before this change; the collection of "To prove it" prints the share of skipped attempts with it, and until a rule exists the "Skipped" column of the flake report is to be read next to the rate. *(Added later: the rule exists since [ADR-135a](flake.md), which budgets the share after the collections with this classifier measured 12.5 % to 25 % of the attempts on the macOS runner.)* (c) A forced climb that the classifier does not recognise (one that comes 7.75 s or more after the step down, or on a load whose counters read zero) fails as before, so the residual rate is not zero, and the cliff of ADR-097 (b) is untouched. (d) The classifier takes a change that the hook records as `abr` and that comes sooner than the interval for "not a decision": a change forced by something other than the protection or a failed request would be skipped as well; the hook cannot tell a decision from a ban (it records both as `abr`: ADR-095, library need), and the check that no response of the origin failed is what narrows that.

**To prove it.** On the macOS runner, `measure.yml`, task `flake-collect`: `--runs 40 --grep PB-ABR-002` (the retry on, as in CI), expecting no first-attempt failure and the invalid runs printed and counted, each reason saying how long after the playhead entered the first 240p segment the climb came (the hypothesis, tested on every one). If a first attempt still fails, its signature says what else there is. Then `--grep PB-ABR-001` and `--grep PB-ABR-006` (`@release`, so in no collection yet), to see whether the same mechanism reaches PB-ABR-001, which this change leaves alone, and how often it reaches PB-ABR-006, which this change covers on the evidence of one failure in Firefox.

**Defending it.** "A test that asserts nothing moves cannot be evaluated on an attempt in which something that is not the subject moved it. Skipping that attempt, loudly and with its numbers, is the honest way to say so; passing it would be false evidence and failing it would be a false alarm. And the skip is only as wide as the evidence: a climb, above the carried rung, inside the interval, with frames dropped and no failed request, which a real flap does not match."

**Amendment 3: PB-ABR-001 ends the same way, in about four attempts of five on the macOS runner, and what proves the change forced is in the origin's log.**

*What failed.* Two collections of `flake-collect --grep PB-ABR-001` on the GitHub macOS runner (macos-15, 3 vCPU, Apple M1 virtual, Playwright 1.64.1's WebKit, one worker, one retry): an isolated job (run 41892727961, 30 repetitions, commit e0a5be3: 7 passed, 4 flaky, 19 failed on both attempts) and a contended one (40 repetitions, commit 30b7d6d, with other macOS jobs of the author running: 8, 6 and 26). The first attempt failed in 23 of 30 repetitions (77 %) and 32 of 40 (80 %), 55 of 70 together (95 % interval 68 % to 87 %); the two jobs do not differ (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.78), so the load of other jobs is not what sets the rate. All 100 failing attempts (42 and 58) kept a trace and all 100 failed the one assertion, `rung changes in the 12 s after reaching 720p: none`. In the isolated job PB-ABR-002 ran after it in each repetition and ended 5 of 30 as invalid runs (17 %) and 25 as passes, none as a failure. The nightlies had shown it before, less: of five runs whose macOS artifacts could be read the first attempt failed in two (41891341097 and 41799187688), and both passed on the retry; in three it passed at once.

*The fingerprint*, read from the traces of 13 attempts (11 of the collections and the 2 of the nightlies) and, in the last row, of all 100 of the collections. *What the 100 have in common besides the table.* The first change after the climb to 720p is a downshift to 360p by `abr` (100 of 100; in 99 it is the only change, in one a change to 240p followed one poll later, 1.95 s). The link is the 20,000 kbps throttle, which carries the top rung. No response of the origin failed and none was cut (the one or two requests the player cancelled at the switch are `client-closed`), no error was recorded, no rebuffer, and the state was `playing`. Apart from the requests for segment 10 that the switch cut, the last segment the origin had delivered had been delivered 3.1 to 3.6 s before the change, because the buffer was full. So the change was not a decision of the adaptation: Shaka decides on the progress of a segment download (`segmentDownloaded` of the ABR manager, gated by the switch interval), and none was progressing. And it was a forced switch: in 100 of 100 the origin was asked **twice for audio segment 10** (for 8 in the nightly attempt whose first 720p segment was the third), the first request cut at the switch without a byte in 86 and finished in 14, and the request for the 720p segment 10 was cancelled by the player in all 100. `Player.disableStream` switches with `force`; `StreamingEngine.switchInternal_` then switches the audio stream again, although it does not change, and `makeAbortDecision_` cancels the request in flight (on a link of 20 Mbps it does). The adaptation's own switch does not: `switchInternal_` returns at once for an audio stream that is already active. A switch of that kind with an audio request in flight is in the traces of the table of this ADR: the step down to 240p of run 6, at 28.38 s, with audio segment 17 in flight from 0.13 s before to 0.12 s after it, left no repeat in its log.

*What is established, and what is not.* Of the callers of `Player.disableStream` in Shaka 5.3.3 (the dropped-frame protection of the ABR manager; and, in the streaming engine, the handling of a failed request or append, which raises an error, and of the media source's quota, which needs a long run of `QuotaExceededError`s of the append, the buffering scale falling from 100 % to 4 % first, with about 3.5 MB of media delivered in the whole load) the protection is the only one that fits a load with no failed request and no error: the elimination of ADR-095, and here the log shows the forced switch itself. **Confirmed as to timing and as to the forced switch; not observed as to the trigger.** What is constant is the time since the playhead entered the first 720p segment, 1.88 to 2.02 s in the 100; within the collections the media time of the change varies as much, because the first 720p segment is always the fifth, and the nightly attempt shows that it is the entry that sets the time: the climb came at media time 0.00, the first 720p segment started at 4 s, and the downshift came at 5.91 s, 1.91 s after it. That is the 2 s period of the protection's poll (`abr.advanced.droppedFramesInterval`), the same moment as the climbs of PB-ABR-002 (1.89 to 1.92 s after the entry into the first 240p segment), with the same caveat: the next segment falls due on the same 2 s lattice, so the phase cannot tell the poll from the fetch. **How the protection fires on so few frames is not explained.** 74 of the 100 attempts dropped fewer than 9 frames in the whole load, 7 of them 2, and 15 % of a 2 s window of 60 frames is 9. Five passing attempts of the macOS leg of the nightlies dropped 18 of 365, 10 of 367, 6 of 385, 11 of 382 and 7 of 386 and held the rung, and so did a Chromium attempt that dropped 2 of 365: the count of the load does not tell the failing attempts from the passing ones. Shaka compares the frames dropped and decoded since its last look, and moves that baseline at every poll, at every `resize` of the video, after every decision that passes the gate of the switch interval, and at `configure`. The traces place none of these closer than 1.9 s before the change: the entry's `resize`, which the hook does not record, is the nearest; no download progressed to open a decision; and ABR-001 calls no `configure` after the load. A poll window with a small decoded count right after a rung change is therefore not shown by them, and none is assumed. What would show it is `getVideoPlaybackQuality()` read every 100 ms, with the `resize` events, through the first 14 s on the runner; it was not run (no browser was run for this amendment).

*Decision.* (1) PB-ABR-001 ends as an invalid run when `forcedDownshift` ([`tests/e2e/abr/bans.ts`](../../tests/e2e/abr/bans.ts), pure, with unit tests on the 13 real timelines and on the cases that must still fail) finds, in the first change after the player reached the top rung, all of: it is made by the player itself (`abr`) and the change before it is not the page's; it goes from the rung the link carries to one below it (the link is 20,000 kbps; a link that carries less is another claim); it came no sooner than the playhead entering the first segment of that rung and no later than 2.25 s after (`FIRST_POLL_WINDOW_S`: the 2 s period of the poll and the 0.25 s of jitter that `DECISION_JITTER_S` allows elsewhere; the 100 measured 1.88 to 2.02 s, so the bound leaves 0.23 s, and a later poll is another story that fails); the load dropped at least one frame; no response of the origin failed and the player raised no error; **and the origin was asked twice for an audio segment**. (2) The last condition is the one the first design lacked, and it is not decoration. The interval cannot prove, as it does for the climbs of PB-ABR-002, that the change was not a decision: the downshift comes 9.2 to 9.4 s after the climb, more than the 8 s of the switch interval (12 of the 13 rows; the 5.93 s of the nightly is the exception). The timing cannot either: the first download after the climb plus 8 s is the one for segment 10, which falls due at the same instant as the poll, so a real decision of the estimate (a regression of exactly what the test exists to catch) would sit in the same window, and timing, frames and no failed request alone would have skipped it. A repeated audio segment is what a forced switch leaves and a decision does not. (3) The reason starts with `INVALID_RUN_PREFIX` and its numbers (`invalid run: forced downshift to 360p 1.9 s after entering 720p, 7 of 398 frames dropped`: 88 or 89 characters, inside the 90 of a report cell), and says that the protection *fits*, which is the status of the cause. The spec ends the attempt with the same annotation, attachment and skip as PB-ABR-002 and PB-ABR-006, after its check of the bandwidths that the variants declare (still made) and before its window assertion; the log is read only when the timeline shows a change after the rung was reached, so an attempt that holds the rung runs as it did. No limit of any assertion changed. (4) The other engines pass as they did (9 of 9 attempts in three nightlies, eight of them with no frame dropped and one, Chromium's, with 2 of 365, which held the rung); the classifier is reached only by an attempt in which the rung changes. (5) Each condition, both bounds and the jitter of the tolerance, the direction, the rule of the first change and the prefix were mutated, 22 mutations, and a test fails without each of them; two survived the first set of tests (the player having to be on the rung the link carries, and the kind filter of the log helper) and got tests of their own.

*What it can hide.* (a) A change of the player that makes the page drop more frames, or the protection fire more, is skipped on the macOS leg in the same way, and the ABR-001 of that leg cannot see it. (b) A forced downshift that does not show every condition (no audio segment in flight at the change, a poll later than 2.25 s after the entry, a load that dropped no frame) fails as before, so on a runner whose phase differs the red would not be zero. The phase was constant in the 102 attempts read, but not in every load of this runner: one of the five invalid runs of PB-ABR-002 in the isolated job, a climb to 720p with 77 of 1,020 frames dropped, came 2.8 s after the entry into the first 240p segment, where the other four came 1.9 s. (c) A real flap of the adaptation from the top rung is not skipped, on a machine that drops frames or not, because it leaves no repeated audio segment; one that happens at the same moment as a ban cannot be told from it: the ban comes first and decides. (d) The share: **on this runner ABR-001 evaluates its claim in about one repetition in five.**

*The budget, as designed.* 77 % and 80 % of the first attempts ending as invalid runs is far above the 40 % of ADR-135a. After 20 scheduled executions of PB-ABR-001 on the macOS leg in the nightly history the flake gate will say `over-invalid-budget` for it (a leg at 78.6 % is over the budget in more than 99.99 % of the windows of 30). That is the designed outcome and not a bug: the rule exists to say that a leg evaluates nothing, and it says so. What to do is the owner's decision: declare the leg a documented gap with this evidence, retire the test on that leg, or give the leg a runner that decodes 720p VP9 (a larger one); not widen the budget and not quarantine the test (ADR-135a).
