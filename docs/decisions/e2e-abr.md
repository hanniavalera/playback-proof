# The adaptive-bitrate specs decisions (ADR-095 to ADR-097)

Architecture decision records for the seven ABR specs of the lab, [`tests/e2e/abr.spec.ts`](../../tests/e2e/abr.spec.ts) (PB-ABR-001 to PB-ABR-007), and the helpers they stand on, [`tests/e2e/abr/`](../../tests/e2e/abr) (the ladder and what a link carries, the windows derived from Shaka's switch interval, the bound on the first frame, the comparison of the player's history with the origin's log, the sentence in front of a failed wait), with their unit tests in [`tests/unit/e2e-abr`](../../tests/unit/e2e-abr). They are written so each one can be defended in a review: what the situation was, what was chosen, what was rejected, what it costs, and how I would argue for it. They build on ADR-005 (events with a cursor), ADR-064 (configuration A is Shaka's defaults, and its first rung depends on the engine), ADR-070 to ADR-079 in [e2e.md](e2e.md) (the support library, the documented gaps, the diagnostics) and ADR-052 in [server-delivery.md](server-delivery.md) (the throttle). The table of the profiles is in [TEST_STRATEGY.md](../../TEST_STRATEGY.md) section 11.

**Where the numbers come from.** One machine: Apple M2 Pro (12 cores, shared with other agents' browsers and containers: the load average was between 7 and 21 during the runs), macOS 27.2, Node 22.11.0, Playwright 1.64.1, Chromium 156.0.8078.4 and WebKit 27.2 natively, Firefox 157.0 and Linux WebKit 27.2 in the Playwright image through `npm run docker:pw -- --cpus 3`, two workers, on 2026-11-04 (UTC), with the specs as committed on the branch `p3/abr` (the two blocks below differ only in the sentences of the failure messages and in the annotations). All seven specs together, 2 workers, `--repeat-each`: two blocks of five runs in a row in Chromium (70 runs), two blocks of three in a row in each of macOS WebKit, Firefox and Linux WebKit (42 runs each). **Chromium 70 of 70, WebKit 42 of 42, Linux WebKit 42 of 42, Firefox 41 of 42**: the one failure is PB-ABR-006 in the first Firefox block; ten more runs of it and the whole second block passed (ADR-097, with its record in [`docs/results/e2e-abr`](../results/e2e-abr/firefox-abr-006-flake.json)). Every spec records what it measured as a `measured` annotation of its result, so the margin to each bound is in the JSON report of a run. Nothing was measured on a CI runner, on x64 or on a GitHub macOS runner. The specs assert behaviour, not these numbers.

## ADR-095: A condition of an ABR test is a finite link derived from the ladder, and a recovery replaces the throttle instead of removing it

**Context.** The catalog names the conditions in words: "an unconstrained network" (PB-ABR-001), "the throttle is removed once the downshift has happened" (PB-ABR-003). On the lab's origin with no rule a segment of 75 kB arrives in a fraction of a millisecond (the log's `totalMs` for the 240p segments after the rule was removed: 0.1 to 1.3 ms). Shaka's `SimpleAbrManager.segmentDownloaded` ignores a download that took less than `abr.cacheLoadThreshold` (5 ms by default) and treats it as a cache hit: no sample reaches the estimator. Whether an engine's estimate hears of a fast network therefore depends on how slow that engine's fetch path is, which is a property of the machine and the engine, not of the player's adaptation. Measured on this machine, with the first literal reading of PB-ABR-003 (`bandwidth-drop`, the rule removed right after the step down): **Chromium stayed on 240p for the 28.6 s it was watched** (media time 16.4 s to 45.0 s: no `variant-changed` at all; in a mutation run of the spec, below, the title played to its end on 240p); with `abr.cacheLoadThreshold: 0` the same Chromium stepped up 9.6 s after the removal (360p, then 720p 8 s later); **macOS WebKit stepped up** on the plain removal, 9.6 s after it to 360p and 27.3 s after it to 720p. One condition, two verdicts, and the player is the same.

**Decision.** (1) "Unconstrained" is a throttle of **20,000 kbps** (10.8 times the top variant's declared 1,845 kbps): finite, so that the 164 kB segment of 360p takes 65 ms and every download is long enough to be measured, and far above anything the ladder needs. (2) A recovery **replaces** the throttle rule by one at **6000 kbps** (3.2 times the top variant), in one `setFaults` call, so the lowest rung's 75 kB segment takes 100 ms. (3) What a link "carries" is derived from the catalog's declared bandwidths (`bestRungCarriedBy(ladder, kbps)`), never typed: PB-ABR-002 settles on the best rung of a 600 kbps link, PB-ABR-006 asserts that this is the lowest rung, PB-ABR-005 asserts that the pinned rung's declared bandwidth is more than its 1500 kbps link, PB-ABR-003 and PB-ABR-001 assert that their links carry the whole ladder. A change of the ladder fails there, with the numbers, instead of making a test pass or fail for the wrong reason.

**Alternatives considered.** Remove the rule as the catalog says: it is red in Chromium and green in WebKit for a reason that belongs to the lab's network model (the finding is reported, ADR-097 and the report). Put `abr.cacheLoadThreshold: 0` in the test (it changes the player under test: configuration A is Shaka's defaults, ADR-064). Use `clean` for PB-ABR-001 (on that profile the climb of WebKit and Firefox depends on their fetch taking more than 5 ms; it climbed in every run here, and nothing guarantees it on a faster machine). Slow the origin by a latency rule (it delays the first byte, which the estimator counts as download time only for the first byte of a response; it does not make the transfer measurable).

**Consequences.** Two numbers in the spec file, each with its arithmetic. A deviation from the catalog's wording, reported as a correction. A 20 Mbps link is not "no link": PB-ABR-002 and PB-ABR-007 start on the profile `bandwidth-drop`, whose first 8 s are unthrottled, and they are written so that it does not matter (ADR-096). On the recovered link the engines agree, which they did not on the removed rule: from the recovery to the first step up, Chromium 8.0 to 9.9 s, WebKit 8.0 to 9.6 s, Firefox 8.0 to 8.1 s, Linux WebKit 7.8 to 9.9 s (the bound of the spec is 14 s).

**Defending it.** "The catalog says what the member experiences; the test has to say it with a network the player can measure. A network that answers in a tenth of a millisecond is not a better network, it is one that Shaka is built to ignore, and I can show the two engines disagreeing about it."

## ADR-096: The oracle is an event or a request, never the clock; the windows are derived from Shaka's interval, and the start rung is nobody's business but the engine's

**Context.** Adaptation runs on seconds: Shaka decides at most every `abr.switchInterval` (8 s, configuration A has no override), when a segment download completes or makes progress, from the minimum of two moving averages weighted by download time. Its first rung is the engine's (Chromium 720p from `navigator.connection.downlink`, WebKit and Firefox 360p), and what it does next is not one pattern: under `bandwidth-drop` the step to 240p is one decision in 8 of 10 Chromium runs (at 16.2 to 16.5 s in 6, 8 s after the evaluation that preceded the drop, and at 10.0 s in 2: Shaka's start-up shortcut lets the first decision through at once when its estimate becomes good only after the link has weakened) and two decisions (720p to 360p to 240p, 8 s apart) in the other 2; two decisions in 10 of 14 WebKit runs, and in every one of the 6 Firefox and 6 Linux WebKit runs. A test that sleeps, or that expects the sequence 720p, 240p, fails on the machine that was not the author's.

**Decision.** (1) Every wait is an event with a cursor (`variant-changed` with a `detail` filter, or the first request of a rung in the log) with a generous timeout that is a bound on a failure and never the pace of a test, derived where it can be (the step down: 8 s until the link weakens + two decisions 8 s apart + one 720p segment in flight at 600 kbps, 6 s, + margin = 45 s). (2) Where the claim is that nothing happens, a window bounded by a positive event closes it, in media time, derived from the interval: **the interval plus two segments (12 s)** (`settleWindowSeconds`), because nothing can change before the interval has elapsed and the manager decides on a segment download; a manager that flaps shows inside it. The first step up is bounded by the interval plus three segments (14 s; measured 8.0 to 9.8 s). (3) The start rung is never asserted. PB-ABR-001 waits for "the top rung" however the player got there (Chromium starts on it: the claim that counts there is "stays"), PB-ABR-002 for the rung the link carries, PB-ABR-004 chooses a target different from the current rung, PB-ABR-005 pins after a few seconds of playback so that the pin is the first thing that moves the rung, and PB-ABR-006 derives its bound for the rung it started on. (4) Every claim about a rung is made on both channels: the player's events and, independently, the origin's log: "from the first request of the rung on, no other video segment was asked for" and "the last three segments delivered are of that rung". The line is drawn at the first request of the new rung, not at a time, because the request that is in flight when the player switches may be of the old rung. (5) A pass that consists of "nothing changed" is protected against vacuity: PB-ABR-005 requires the origin to show a pinned-rung segment that took longer than its 2 s of playing time, so that it cannot pass on a lab that applied no throttle. (6) PB-ABR-006's first frame is bounded by what the link must carry before it, **at most 251 kB** on a start that is too heavy for the link (128 kB that Shaka must sample before it trusts its estimate, two 16 kB chunks of the throttle's grants, the lowest rung's first segment, the audio's, the inits) at 600 kbps less the throttle's 10 % tolerance, **3.7 s, plus 2 s** for everything that is not the link (six times the slowest unthrottled first frame of the smoke runs): 5.7 s, against 2.8 to 3.7 s measured in four engines. (7) PB-ABR-004 plays through the first segment of the new rung, because Shaka keeps what is buffered and applies the choice to what is fetched next: the new resolution reaches the playhead about 10 s after the click, and that is where a broken change breaks.

**Alternatives considered.** Asserting a rung sequence or a switch count (wrong, above). `expect.poll` or fixed windows in seconds (the interval is Shaka's wall clock, the window must be media time, and both must survive a loaded machine). A predicate such as "any rung above 240p" in the wait (the page boundary cannot carry a function, ADR-070: the first change after the cursor is read instead, and the spec checks that it is a step up). A tighter bound on the first frame (the bound is thin by design for one regression: with the player made to trust its estimate only after 1 GB, which means it fetches the whole 720p segment, the first frame is at 5.87 s against the bound of 5.72 s, so the margin that catches that regression is 0.15 s).

**Consequences.** The tests are slow and say "it ended on 240p", not "how fast it got there": speed is the performance gate's (PB-PERF-003). The wide bounds would not catch a step down that takes 40 s where it takes 17. A mutation that did not turn PB-ABR-002 red: `abr.switchInterval: 60`, because in Chromium Shaka's first good estimate arrives after the link weakened and its start-up shortcut (`lastTimeChosenMs -= switchInterval`) then allows the decision at once. The ABR tests record the dropped frames of the load in the message of a rung change (`droppedFramesNote`): Shaka also leaves a rung when 15 % of the frames of a 2 s window are dropped, whatever the link.

**Defending it.** "The player's adaptation has a clock of its own. I wait for what it says it did and I check what the origin saw it do; the windows are the interval it is bound by, so when a window is too short the arithmetic says why, not a feeling about this machine."

## ADR-097: PB-ABR-007 compares two accounts as a story, and says what is left unresolved

**Context.** The hook is ours, and its history feeds the baselines and the experiments (`bitrate.switches`, `bitrate.history`). The origin's log is the independent account of which rung was fetched. Comparing two numbers (a count) agrees by accident: the history `720p, 240p` and a log of `720p, 360p, 240p` have different counts, but `720p, 360p` and `720p, 240p` have the same one.

**Decision.** `compareHistoryWithLog` (a pure function with 12 unit tests) compares the **rung story**. The video segment requests of the log become runs of one rung each, in arrival order. The history entries are matched to the runs as a subsequence: the origin may not have seen a rung the player never named, or in another order, and the player may name a rung the origin never saw a segment of only if it left it within one segment duration (Firefox starts on 360p and is on 720p 12 ms later; no segment of the first can have been asked for) and never the rung it ends on. For each change the player made itself (`abr` or `manual`; the initial selection is exempt, because the hook records it once the load has resolved, a moment after the first requests) the first request of the new rung must not be earlier than the player's timestamp, and at most one segment after it. The two clocks are aligned by the one event both saw, the manifest request: the page's `load-requested` came before it and the origin received it before the page's `manifest-loaded`, so the alignment is uncertain by `metrics.startup.manifestMs` (the spec reads it from the page: a few ms on a quiet machine), plus 25 ms for the resolution of the clocks. "One segment": Shaka cuts the request in flight when the new segment is the smaller (`shouldAbortCurrentRequest_`) and otherwise lets it finish, which is under one segment of the new rung (1.2 s at 600 kbps); with a full buffer the next fetch waits at most one segment of playback. `metrics.bitrate.switches` must equal the length of the history less one.

**Measured.** The first request of a new rung comes −118 to +509 ms from the player's timestamp aligned by the manifest (Chromium −1 to +12 ms, WebKit −6 to +509, Firefox −118 to +41, Linux WebKit −40 to +93): the negative part is the uncertainty of the alignment, the positive part the time the page takes to issue the fetch, and the +509 ms is WebKit's climb to 720p half a second into the playback, when the buffer was already full of 360p and the next fetch waited for the playhead (the full-buffer case of the one-segment bound). The comparison was made to fail by stamping the history 3 s late: "the origin was asked for v240 at 16919 ms, 2951 ms before the player says it switched to 240p (abr, not before 19870 ms of the origin's clock): the player's timestamp is late".

**Alternatives considered.** Comparing counts (above). Comparing the time of the switch without the alignment (the two clocks have different origins). Aligning the clocks with `performance.timeOrigin` of the page (a spec does not evaluate code in the page, ADR-070; and the container's and the host's clocks are the same machine's, but only the manifest anchor needs no assumption). A one-segment tolerance at the start (the start is exempt for a reason, not forgiven).

**Consequences and what is not proved.** A hook that is wrong in the way the origin's log is wrong cannot be found by comparing them (the log is the service's, tested by PB-LAB-001). Nothing here says which rung was *shown*: the hook says selected, the origin says fetched, and the decoder's picture is not observed (ADR-069). The `reason` of a change cannot be checked against the origin.

**Unresolved, with the evidence.** (a) PB-ABR-006 failed once in 16 runs in Firefox in the image (3 + 10 + 3), on the loaded machine: 2.2 s after the step down to 240p, and well inside the 8 s interval, the player moved *up* to 360p, a rung a 600 kbps link cannot carry, and rebuffered three times. 13 of 366 decoded frames were dropped in the load (the hook does not say when); Shaka's dropped-frame protection (`abr.droppedFrames`, default on) disables the playing variant for 30 s when 15 % of a 2 s window is dropped, is not held back by the switch interval and leaves 360p and 720p to choose from. That fits, it was not reproduced, and it is a finding about configuration A on slow links and weak machines, not about the test: [`docs/results/e2e-abr/firefox-abr-006-flake.json`](../results/e2e-abr/firefox-abr-006-flake.json). The spec now reports the rung change before the rebuffers and names the dropped frames. (b) PB-ABR-002 in WebKit on macOS rebuffered once in 4 of 14 runs (never twice): in the four runs where the first decision came at 16.7 s or later and went through 360p (which the link does not carry) for 8 s. The assertion is the catalog's, at most one, and these runs are at its limit; in Chromium, Firefox and Linux WebKit no run rebuffered. (c) Chromium on `bandwidth-drop` makes its first decision about 16.5 s into the playback with about 3.4 s of media buffered (read from the log: segment 10 of 2 s each ends at 20 s), and the 240p segment it then needs takes 1.1 s at 600 kbps: by that arithmetic a decision later than about 18.9 s would stall it. It came at 16.2 to 16.9 s in every run seen, so the margin of about 2 s is real, and it comes from the phase between the link's 8 s and Shaka's own 8 s grid: a coincidence of the profile, not a property of the player.

**Defending it.** "Two instruments that agree to a few milliseconds on when it happened and in what order are a better argument for the instrument than any unit test of it. The test says what it does not know: it cannot say which rung was on the screen."
