# The VOD suites decisions (ADR-092 to ADR-094)

Architecture decision records for the playback specs of video on demand: [`tests/e2e/vod-lifecycle.spec.ts`](../../tests/e2e/vod-lifecycle.spec.ts) (PB-VOD-003 play to the end, PB-VOD-004 pause, PB-VOD-005 resume, PB-VOD-010 seek into the last segment, PB-VOD-011 play after ended), [`tests/e2e/vod-seek.spec.ts`](../../tests/e2e/vod-seek.spec.ts) (PB-VOD-006 seek inside the buffer, PB-VOD-007 seek to an unbuffered position, PB-VOD-008 seek back to a position the buffer no longer holds, PB-VOD-009 five rapid seeks) and [`tests/e2e/vod-metrics.spec.ts`](../../tests/e2e/vod-metrics.spec.ts) (PB-VOD-012 metrics integrity, PB-VOD-013 the open-codec path), with their helpers in [`tests/e2e/vod/`](../../tests/e2e/vod) and the unit tests of those helpers in [`tests/unit/e2e-vod`](../../tests/unit/e2e-vod). PB-VOD-001 and PB-VOD-002 are the support library's ([e2e.md](e2e.md), ADR-070 to ADR-079). They are written so each one can be defended in a review: what the situation was, what was chosen, what was rejected, what it costs, and how I would argue for it. The rules the specs follow are those of [CONTRACTS.md](../CONTRACTS.md) section 7 and of ADR-005 and ADR-074 (waits are events with a cursor; the one way to let time pass is `observeFrames`); the metric definitions are in [METRICS.md](../METRICS.md).

**Where the numbers come from.** One machine: Apple M2 Pro (12 cores, shared with other agents' browsers and containers: `uptime` read load averages of 11.15, 10.76 and 12.28 at 23:15 local time on 2026-11-03, during the stability runs), macOS 27.2, Node 22.11.0, Playwright 1.64.1; Chromium 156.0.8078.4 and WebKit 27.2 natively; Firefox 157.0 and Linux WebKit 27.2 in the Playwright image through `npm run docker:pw -- --cpus 3`; two workers; 2026-11-04 (UTC). The quantities of the first table were recorded by a scratch spec that was not committed (it printed one JSON line per run): five runs in each engine, except Linux WebKit, where one of the five runs failed (I did not capture why; ten more runs of the same scratch spec passed) and four were kept. Nothing in the tables was measured on a CI runner or on x64; the revision paragraph of each record reads the artifacts of the first CI run of the pull request on a GitHub macos-15 runner (3 CPUs, WebKit only, two workers; the merge gate run 41784609878 and the release run 41784609875) and says so. The specs assert behaviour, not these numbers.

## ADR-092: Every number a spec compares a measurement with is derived from the content and from four engines, and lives in one file

**Context.** Eleven specs compare a measurement with a bound: where the clock is after a seek, how far `ended` is from the end of the title, how far a resumed clock is from where it was paused, how many frames two counters may differ by. A bound chosen by feel is either too tight, and a merge gate flakes, or too loose, and the test cannot fail. The lab's rule is that a tolerance is derived and not guessed. The streams are known exactly (30 fps, 2 s segments that are also GOPs, audio that ends up to one 21 ms frame before the video), and the four engines differ in how they report them.

**Decision.** [`tests/e2e/vod/tolerances.ts`](../../tests/e2e/vod/tolerances.ts) is the one place of every such number, each with a comment that says what it was derived from. The rule: a bound is a margin over the largest value measured in any of the four engines, and sits well below the smallest value that the failure it is there to catch would produce. Every seek target is the middle of a segment (`middleOfSegment`), so that the two failures the specs fear (a landing at the start of the GOP, a skip to the next one) are a whole second from the right answer. The bound for a position of the clock is not a distance: the first version used 0.25 s and 0.5 s, five times the noise measured on one machine, and the first CI run showed that no distance is right (revision below); it is the wall clock the clock had to run in, plus a frame, and a whole second from the failures is what lets it separate them.

The difference of the decoded and the presented counters at about 5 s of a first play, in frames, from the `readings` annotation of PB-VOD-012 in the runs of the final series (and the earlier series of that spec): 3 to 7 in Chromium (ten runs; the 7 was the run that dropped 7 frames), 4 to 5 in macOS WebKit (six), minus 2 to minus 1 in Firefox (six: the presented counter ahead), and in Linux WebKit 0 to 12 before the correction below and minus 1 to 1 after it (26 runs). The bound was 10 for the difference of decoded and presented, and is now 15 for the difference of decoded and presented plus dropped (revision below).

**Three bounds were wrong the first time, and real runs said so.** The wall-clock bound of the frame window: I first required the media time of `measureFrames(2)` not to exceed the wall time of the call, and macOS WebKit returned a window of 2000 ms of media time in a call of 1995 ms. The window is anchored at frame callbacks, and the first can report a frame that was presented up to a frame period before the call, so the bound is the wall time plus one frame period (1000 / 30 ms) plus the 2 ms the clocks compare to. And the counters: the presented counter starts at the first frame callback of the load, while the decoded counter counts every frame, so a slow first paint is decoded and never presented. PB-VOD-012 failed in Linux WebKit with the container busy: in the first run of the suite there (presented 122, decoded 152), in 3 of the next 10 runs (presented 144, 131 and 127 against decoded 158, 155 and 153: differences of 14, 24 and 26 frames), and in 1 of the 10 after those (153 against 164). In the one failure whose timeline I kept, the first frame was presented at media time 0.346 s (0.34 s after `playing`), and in a sixteen-run scratch the first frame came 0.02 to 0.45 s of media time into the play. Adding back `round(30 x the media time of the firstframe event)` explains the difference to within one frame in the nine of those sixteen runs whose output I kept (minus 1 to 0, against 0 to 14 raw), and after the correction the same test passed 12 of 12 runs in that engine, and its later runs show minus 1 to 1 in 26 of 26. What is left after the correction is the decoder running ahead of the presentation, which is why the agreement bound was 10 frames (the first version; the revision below counts the dropped frames and sets 15): a third of a second of video, 1.4 times the largest difference left (7, Chromium with 7 frames dropped), and a fraction of what a counter that doubles, repeats or restarts would show (a second of video or more). And the frame rate of the window: I first required it to be within 10 % of the stream's 30 fps, and in one run of the stability series Linux WebKit, with the container busy, presented 46 frames in the 2 s window (23 fps) and decoded 46 too: the counters agreed with each other and with the clock, the machine was slow, and the bound failed a correct instrument. The window now has an upper bound (a page cannot present more than 110 % of the stream's frames: a counter that double counts) and a lower bound of half the stream's rate (the instrument is alive; a counter that undercounts by half also fails the agreement of the two counters), and whether a machine holds 30 fps is what the performance baselines measure on a quiet one.

**Alternatives considered.** Round numbers chosen once and kept (the failure above would have been read as an instrument fault). Relative bounds (a percentage of the media time: the fixed cost of a dispatch is what matters, not the length of the title). A bound calibrated at run time against the engine under test (the oracle would be set by the thing it judges, and it could never fail). One tolerance per engine (a spec must not decide by the browser's name: the bound is the one that holds in all four, and what differs is reported).

**Consequences.** The bounds are valid for these streams and this encoder: a change of the content hash of `GET /api/streams` (a different ffmpeg build) is a reason to measure again, and the table is the procedure. A defect smaller than the margin passes: a seek that lands 0.2 s off, or a resume that moves the clock 0.4 s, is not caught when the call took longer than that. The exact comparison of a paused clock is the exception on purpose (it was 0 in every engine, so any movement is a failure) and is guarded by a positive control (ADR-094). A main-thread stall between `play()` and the `playing` event moves the media time that is read by the same amount, and since the revision below it no longer fails the resume check, because the bound is the wall clock of the call.

**Defending it.** "Each number in that file has a reason you can check and a measurement next to it. When one of them fails, the message says the distance and the bound, and the table says whether the distance is noise or a defect. The two that were wrong were wrong in the runs, not in review, and now say why."

**Revision: positions of the clock are judged by the wall clock they had to run in, not by a distance (the first CI run of the pull request: GitHub macos-15, 3 CPUs, WebKit, two workers).** The four bounds above that were distances in seconds (0.25 for a landing, 0.5 for a wait, a resume and a restart) held on the M2 Pro and in every Linux leg, and failed on the runner. Merge gate: PB-VOD-007 read `seeked` at 41.4 s for a target of 41 s in both attempts (0.4 against 0.25), PB-VOD-009 at 37.3 s for 37 s in its first, PB-VOD-006 returned 11.414 s from a wait for 8 s in its first (3.41 against 0.5), and with one worker (the orchestrator's re-run) it did once more, at 9.91 s. Release run: PB-VOD-009 returned 39.92 s from a wait for 38 s (1.92 against 0.5). The artifacts say that two different things happened, and that neither is a defect of the seek. **A wait is served late.** PB-VOD-006's timeline has `seeked` at page time 6480 ms (7 s) and the next event of the page at 10866 ms with the clock at 11.148 s: the clock ran 4.1 s of media in 4.4 s of wall clock, at rate 1 as it must, and the call for 8 s was answered when the page got to it, at 11.414 s. **A landing is moved by the player.** PB-VOD-007's timeline has the seek to 41 s at 3715 ms, then three more `seeking` events that nobody asked for (`requested: false`) at 41.1, 41.2 and 41.3 s, 1.0 to 1.1 s apart, a fourth at 41.4 s, and `seeked` at 15218 ms, 11.5 s after the seek began; PB-VOD-009's last seek went from 37 to 37.3 s in 4.3 s (4413 to 8667 ms) and in the release run from 37 to 37.2 s in 2.3 s. Shaka 5.3.3 has a stall detector on by default (`streaming.stallEnabled`, `stallThreshold` 1 s, `stallSkip` 0.1 s: read from the installed build), which adds 0.1 s to `currentTime` for every second the playhead has not moved, and a seek that waits seconds for a starved media pipeline is moved by it. Both are statements about the machine, which no constant can be: 0.5 s is wrong at the first starved second, and a number that fits the runner is blind on a fast machine.

The bound that is sound is about time. Between two readings of the media clock of a player at rate 1 the clock advanced no more than the wall clock did, plus a frame period and the resolution of the clocks (`expectClockPace`; and, since the second revision below, the step the clock takes when it restarts), and did not go back by more than a frame. The wall clock is the test process's (`stopwatch`), started before the call that began the span and read after the reading that ended it, so it brackets the page whenever the page got to it. The page's own timestamps cannot replace it: a page that is blocked records `seeking` and `seeked` in one burst, with the page clock between them at 0 and the media clock having run, and the variant that used the page clock alone refused exactly that case in the unit tests. They are used where they are sound: the two events carry the media time and the page time of the same instant, so `seeked` cannot read more than `seeking` plus the page time between them, which refuses a skip that the wall bracket, widened by slow round trips, would let through. A landing at the start of the segment is a second before its target and a skip to the next segment is a second ahead of a bracket of 0.2 to 0.4 s, so both still fail; `tests/unit/e2e-vod/helpers.test.ts` holds the numbers of the CI run as cases, with a lag-free landing, a seeked recorded late (alone and in a burst), the nudged landings of PB-VOD-007 and PB-VOD-009, a landing a second early, a skip, and the wait of PB-VOD-006. Five mutations of the helpers turn them red: the old absolute bounds (0.25 and 0.5: five and four cases), no lower bound (the landing a second early and the rewound resume), no page readings (the skip under a wide bracket), the page clock as the only wall clock (the late seeked), and the old frame comparison (ADR-094). The resume and restart checks use the same function (`from` the paused position, `from` 0), so a `playing` event read late is no longer a failure.

**What it does not see.** A skip shorter than the wall clock of the call: after a wait for one second of media the bracket is about 1.1 to 1.5 s, which is what the 0.5 s bound was; on a starved runner a jump up to the starvation fits in the bracket. `waitForMediaTime` returns only a media time, not the page time at which it read it, so the continuation check has no page clock to use: a wait that returns both is a library need. **The other constants were read for the same assumption and left alone.** `END_TOLERANCE_SEC`: the clock stops at the end, so a late `ended` reads the same position (the CI run read 12 s of a 12 s title). `MIN_CONTROL_ADVANCE_SEC` and `OBSERVED_FRAMES`: a starved page paints its frames later, so the clock advances more across them, never less, and the paused clock is compared for equality. `EVICTION_PLAY_SEC` and `UNBUFFERED_FROM_SEC`: premises of a seek made a few seconds in, true for a playhead below 4 s, and the timelines of the CI run have the seeks made at 1.9 to 2.1 s. `FPS_MAX_SHARE`, `FPS_MIN_SHARE` and `FRAME_WINDOW_SEC`: the window is media time and the presented frames of the same two callbacks, so a late callback moves both (the CI window read 28.8 fps). The startup bounds are each against a clock that brackets the player (the origin's and the process's). The one that was wrong besides the four was `FRAME_AGREEMENT_TOLERANCE` (ADR-094).

**Second revision: the clock is not wall-synchronous right after a restart (the nightly of the next pull request, run 41793007785: ubuntu-24.04, Chromium, two workers).** The wall-clock bound failed PB-VOD-005 in both attempts in Chromium, and no other VOD spec failed on any leg of that nightly (macOS WebKit included): "media time of the playing event after the resume: it is past the 3.000375 s the clock started from by more than the 10 ms of wall clock it had to run in ... Received 3.060896", and on the retry "past the 2.991299 s ... by more than the 8 ms ... Received 3.040366". The events of the two attempts give the step itself: `paused` at 3.000375 s and `playing` at 3.060896 s (60.5 ms ahead), `paused` at 2.991299 s and `playing` at 3.040366 s (49.1 ms ahead), with 10 and 8 ms of wall clock between the call and the event. The clock steps forward by 50 to 60 ms when it starts ticking again, and is wall-synchronous from then on. My own first table already held it (`playing` 0.050 to 0.059 s past the pause in five runs on the M2 Pro: a steady step, not noise): I filed it under the bound of 0.5 s, then replaced that bound with a rule, that the clock cannot run faster than the wall clock, whose unit tests assumed the step away. A pass keeps no timeline (only failures attach `qa-events.json`), so the other engines' margins in this nightly cannot be read; their steps are those of the first table: macOS WebKit 0.2 to 4.2 ms, Firefox 0 in four runs and 101 ms in one, Linux WebKit 12 to 79 ms, and they passed with whatever margin the wall clock of their calls gave them. I did not look into why the clock steps (the audio renderer starting again is the likeliest).

The allowance is `RESTART_CLOCK_STEP_SEC`, 0.15 s, in `tolerances.ts` with that basis: 1.5 times the largest step seen (Firefox's 101 ms), 2.5 times Chromium's, and a seventh of the smallest real defect (the pauses and the targets are in the middle of a segment, so a skip or a rewind is a second away and a seek back is seconds). `expectClockPace` adds it once to the upper bound of every span it judges, because each of them contains a restart of the clock; it is forward only (the smallest step measured was 0), so the bound on going back stays a frame.

**The windows I treated, and why.** (a) The resume: the `playing` event after a pause (PB-VOD-005), where the step was measured. (b) The replay: the `playing` event after `play()` on an ended title (PB-VOD-011), a seek to 0 and a restart; Linux WebKit read up to 74 ms there. (c) The reading of a wait after any restart (`expectClockContinued`, PB-VOD-005 and 006 to 011): a second of play follows the restart and the step is in the reading; the one after a resume is the closest call, since the bracket of a resume holds no seek. (d) The landing, the `seeked` event and the page's own two readings of it (PB-VOD-006 to 010): the clock restarts at the landing, so a handler that reads after it reads the step. The measured landings (0.0003 to 0.0008 s in Chromium, 0 in macOS WebKit and Firefox, up to 0.049 s in Linux WebKit) were inside the old bound, and the step after a seek was not measured; the allowance is there because the bracket of a Chromium seek (20 to 45 ms) is smaller than a resume's step (50 to 60 ms), so a fast seek followed by the same step would fail by a few milliseconds. **Not treated, and why.** PB-VOD-004 has no upper bound on the clock: the paused clock is compared for equality (0 in every engine) and its positive control is a lower bound over a clock that is playing. `ended` (PB-VOD-003 and PB-VOD-010): the clock stops at the end and reads the same whenever it is read. PB-VOD-012's window: media time against the wall clock over two seconds of steady play that starts after `waitForMediaTime(3)`, with no restart in it (2.000 to 2.033 s of media in 1996 to 2200 ms of wall clock, 31 ms or more under a bound that already carries a frame and 2 ms). The startup bounds read no media clock.

**Proof and what is open.** `tests/unit/e2e-vod/helpers.test.ts` has the two Chromium steps with their wall clocks, the largest steps of Firefox and Linux WebKit, the clock read a second after a resume (1.06 s past the pause in 1.01 s of wall clock) and a landing read right after the restart as cases that pass, and a skip of one segment and of two, a rewind to the start of the GOP, a seek back to 0, a clock three seconds ahead after a second and a landing a second ahead within 20 ms of the page clock as cases that fail. Setting the allowance to zero turns the two accepting tests red, which is the failure of the nightly; setting it to 2 s turns the three refusing tests red. Open: the step after a seek, which needs the clock read against the wall clock once the seek is over, and the hook gives no page time with the reading of a wait (a library need already on the list); and the cause of the step.

## ADR-093: The seek specs are judged by the origin's log, with a definition of "again" that survives adaptation, and the recipe for "evicted" is what Shaka really does

**Context.** PB-VOD-006 to PB-VOD-009 are about what the player does with its buffer, and the buffer is invisible to a spec: what a spec can see is the player's events and the origin's request log. Reading Shaka 5.3.3 (`lib/media/streaming_engine.js` in the installed package: `seeked()` and `getSegmentReferenceNeeded_`) and then watching it in two engines changed three assumptions. A seek to a position that is buffered does nothing to the buffer; a seek to one that is not calls `forceClearBuffer_` and starts again from there, so the whole buffer is emptied, not only what `bufferBehind` (30 s) would evict. The first segment fetched after such a seek is looked up `streaming.inaccurateManifestTolerance` (2 s by default) before the target: for a target of 41 s (covered by segment 21) the first request was segment 20 in Chromium and segment 21 in WebKit (I did not record the first request in Firefox or Linux WebKit, and I did not trace why WebKit differs), so an oracle that says "no segment below the one that covers the target is requested after the seek" fails in Chromium on a correct player. And WebKit starts the title on the 360p rung and climbs to 720p about 0.5 to 1 s after the first frame, replacing the segments it had buffered (`v360` 1 and 2, then `v720` 2, in the WebM stream), so "this segment number was requested twice" can happen without a seek.

**Decision.** (1) "Fetched again" has two definitions, each for the row that needs it. A **refetch** is the same segment of the **same representation** requested before and after a point (`refetched`): a seek served from the buffer, or the first fetches after a seek to somewhere new, never repeat one, and a climb of the adaptation (the same number from another rung) is not one. PB-VOD-006 and PB-VOD-007 assert there is none. A segment number **fetched again** from whichever representation (`fetchedAgain`) is how PB-VOD-008 shows that the buffer did not hold a position it had held: the segment that covers 3 s had been fetched at the start and is fetched again after the way back. (2) PB-VOD-007 asserts that the segment that covers the target (and not "the first one") is requested after the seek, and that nothing the player already had is requested again. It does not assert that nothing below the covering segment is requested: that is Shaka's lookup, not a defect, and a player that walked through the gap would show it as a hang or a late landing, which the other assertions catch. The difference between the engines is in this record and in the comment of the spec, and not in an `if`. (3) PB-VOD-006 states its premise from the player (`bufferedAheadSec` at the seek is more than the distance to the target plus a second: it fails with "premise of the test, not the behaviour under test") and checks from the origin that the buffer served the seek. `rebuffer.count` is asserted as the row says, knowing what it cannot see: a stall that starts inside a seek window is excluded by definition ([METRICS.md](../METRICS.md)), so the log is the second evidence. (4) PB-VOD-008 is the obvious recipe (a seek far ahead, three seconds there, a seek back to 3 s: the early segment is fetched again and playback resumes) and it says what removes the data: the seek's own flush. In the runs I recorded, the buffered range after the seek to 41 s was 38.02 to 52.00 s in Chromium and 40.00 to 52.00 s in WebKit, so the early data was gone before `bufferBehind` could evict it. Eviction proper needs about 42 s of continuous play (the first 12 s are evicted once the playhead is 30 s past them) or a walk forward in buffered hops, which I did not build; the observable of the row (the way back needs the origin, and playback resumes) is the same. The natural way not to hard-code Shaka's 30 s is to read it from `qa.info().configDefaults`, but that object holds only the keys that the configuration overrides, and configuration A has none, so it is `{}`: the spec does not need the value, because the refetch proves the premise, and the need is in the report. (5) PB-VOD-009 issues five `seek()` calls of the handle without awaiting (`Promise.allSettled`, so that a seek that never resolves is named), with targets beyond what a player two seconds in has buffered (57, 17, 47, 27 and 37 s of 60: each needs a segment from the origin, so none can finish before the five commands, which reached the page within 1 to 2 ms of each other by the page clock of the `seeking` events, have been given). It asserts that the race was run (no `seeked` before the last `seeking`), that the last one wins (the `seeked` that follows the last `seeking` is at the last target, the clock goes on for one more second, the state is `playing`, the covering segment of the last target was requested) and that there is no error. It does not assert the intermediate events: the engines coalesce differently (Chromium recorded five `seeking` and one `seeked`; WebKit one to three `seeking` and one `seeked`), and the viewer's outcome is the final state. (6) PB-VOD-010 waits for `ended` with a message that says how far the title got and which segments the origin delivered, and PB-VOD-011 proves "without reloading" twice: no `load-requested`, `manifest-loaded`, `loaded` or `unloaded` on the timeline since `play()`, and no manifest request in the origin's log.

**Alternatives considered.** The literal oracle (a failing assertion in Chromium for behaviour that is not a defect). Counting refetches by number (the WebKit climb fails PB-VOD-006 when it comes late). Pinning the rung with `abr: false` for the seek specs (it removes the confounder, and with it the player as members get it; the definition of a refetch is enough). Pausing before the seek of PB-VOD-007 so that no request can be in flight (a different scenario: the player was in its streaming loop at the seek, and the definition tolerates what that loop may have started). A hop-by-hop walk to evict by `bufferBehind` (above). Asserting `playing` after a seek (WebKit does not fire it, METRICS.md).

**Consequences.** PB-VOD-007 and PB-VOD-008 do not tell flush from eviction; if Shaka stopped flushing on a seek, PB-VOD-008 would fail with the message that the player still had the segment, and that would be the signal to build the 42 s variant. A leftover request that the player issued for the old position in the few milliseconds between the cursor and the seek is a new segment and neither a refetch nor a failure. The covering-segment check of PB-VOD-009 reads the log after the clock moved on, so a slow origin makes it wait, not fail. Everything here is about Shaka 5.3.3, configuration A: a change of defaults changes the table of ADR-092, not the rules of the specs.

**Defending it.** "A seek spec that does not know what the player does with its buffer is a guess. I read the code and watched the engines, and found that the obvious oracle would fail on a correct player and that two of its assumptions (eviction, a configuration value to read) were not what Shaka does. The oracle I wrote fails for the defects of the catalog: no covering segment, a refetch, no landing, no resume."

**Revision: a seek on a starved page takes seconds, and the player moves it (the first CI run of the pull request: GitHub macos-15, 3 CPUs, WebKit, two workers).** The seek to 41 s of PB-VOD-007 took 11.5 s from `seeking` to `seeked` (page time 3715 to 15218 ms), and the five seeks of PB-VOD-009 took 4.3 s (2.3 s in the release run), against 36 to 218 ms on the M2 Pro (the table of ADR-092). Nothing in the rules above depends on how long a seek takes: the oracles are the origin's log, the covering segment and the definition of a refetch, and none of them failed in an attempt that reached it. What failed was the landing check, and the revision of ADR-092 says why (a waiting playhead is moved by Shaka's stall detector, 0.1 s for every second) and what replaced it (the wall clock of the call bounds how far the clock can have run). Two premises were read against the timelines. An unbuffered position holds for a playhead below 4 s (`UNBUFFERED_FROM_SEC`), and every seek of the run was made at 1.9 to 2.1 s; a `waitForMediaTime(2)` served seconds late is the one way it could stop holding, and then PB-VOD-009's assertion that the five seeks overlapped (no `seeked` before the last `seeking`) fails with its own message, not as a landing. And in PB-VOD-009 the last `seeking` of the timeline can be one of the player's nudges (the five calls were still pending, so the timeline marks them `requested: true`: 37.1, 37.2 and 37.3 s): the spec takes the `seeked` that follows the last `seeking` as where the clock settled and judges it against the last target, with the wall clock of the five calls as the bracket. WebKit delivered the five seeks as one `seeking` event in the merge gate and two in the release run (27 s and 37 s), as the engines differ in coalescing them (above). A seek that takes 11.5 s fits the 45 s of a test and the 20 s of the hook's `seek()`; one that took longer would be reported by the hook's timeout with what the origin was asked for (`describeOriginSince`).

## ADR-094: The instrument checks itself against clocks and counts it does not own, and a negative observation carries its positive control

**Context.** PB-VOD-012 exists because baselines, A/B verdicts and readiness numbers rest on `window.__qa`, and the hook reads the browser's own counters: `getVideoPlaybackQuality()` for decoded and dropped frames, `requestVideoFrameCallback` for presented ones. A spec that compares the hook with itself proves nothing, and a spec cannot read the browser's counters except through the hook (specs do not `page.evaluate`, ADR-070). What a spec does have that the hook does not own: the stream's catalog (30 fps), the origin's clock (the request log's `msSinceStart` and `totalMs`), and the wall clock of the process that runs the test. PB-VOD-004 and PB-VOD-005 have the opposite problem: they assert that nothing moves, and an assertion that nothing moves passes in a page that does not paint.

**Decision.** PB-VOD-012 plays a clean first play (no seek: after a seek the counters diverge by design, ADR-065) and reads the metrics after a `measureFrames(2)` window. Frames: decoded above zero; dropped no more than decoded; the reported dropped ratio equals dropped over decoded; presented not null; presented plus dropped plus the frames the clock ran through before the first presented frame agrees with decoded within fifteen frames (ADR-092; ten and without the dropped ones in the first version, revision below); the window's effective frame rate is at most 110 % of the stream's 30 fps and at least half of it, its presented and decoded counts agree, and its media time does not exceed the wall time of the call by more than a frame. Startup: defined; equal to the difference of the timeline's own `load-requested` and `firstframe`; the manifest time positive and no more than the load time and the startup; at least the time the origin took to answer the manifest request; the startup at least the origin's span from the arrival of the manifest request to the end of the delivery of the first video segment (the player cannot present a frame before the bytes exist); and at most the wall time of the test from navigation to the first frame (a startup measured from the wrong origin would exceed it). No rebuffer, no error. The readings of every run are an annotation of the result (`readings`), so a drift can be followed in the reports. PB-VOD-004 gives its observation a positive control in the same test: the same 30 frames with the player playing move the clock by at least 0.1 s (0.47 to 1.31 s measured), then the pause is acknowledged by `pause()`, the clock must be exactly where it was when `pause()` resolved (exactly equal: it was in all four engines), and no `playing` event may follow. PB-VOD-005 pauses in the middle of a segment (a second from the GOP boundaries) so that a rewind or a skip is a second from the right answer, and compares the media times of the `paused` and `playing` events and the clock after a second of play. PB-VOD-013 plays `vod-webm` over DASH and checks, besides the first frame and three seconds, that the player's active variant carries the codecs the catalog says the WebM files have (VP9 and Opus) and that every initialization and media segment the origin delivered is a `.webm`; where an engine cannot play the flavour the shared fixture has already skipped the test with the evidence of the codec matrix, and none does today.

**Alternatives considered.** Reading `getVideoPlaybackQuality()` in a page evaluation (the same counter as the hook's, through a path the library forbids). Comparing the counters with a fixed frame count (it depends on the time at which they are read and on the machine). Requiring the presented and decoded counters to agree without the first-frame correction (it failed in a loaded engine for a reason that is the startup's, which `startupMs` already measures). Asserting that the frame rate is 30 and that nothing was dropped on every machine (it measures the machine, as the first version of the window check did; the window now checks the instrument against the stream's ceiling, the agreement of the two counters and `dropped` against `decoded`). A fixed pause time in 004 (forbidden, and a pause is acknowledged by an event). Playing both codecs in one spec (PB-VOD-013 is one row).

**Consequences.** PB-VOD-012 can still fail for a machine so loaded that the page presents fewer than half of the stream's frames, or that its decoded and presented counts drift apart by more than the decoder's lead, and the message then says presented, decoded and dropped. It cannot tell a hook that reads the right counter wrongly from a browser whose counter is wrong in the same way as the hook reads it: for that there would have to be a second reader, and the lab does not have one. It checks the instrument on a first play in DASH; HLS and live use the same hook but are not repeated. The startup lower bound compares two clocks in two processes: the 2 ms of resolution is the margin. PB-VOD-004 and PB-VOD-011 depend on the page's frames (`observeFrames`) and on `ended`, and PB-VOD-003 and PB-VOD-011 play a 12 s title to its end, which is why they are the longest specs (12.5 to 15 s against the estimate of 12 s).

**Defending it.** "The instrument is checked against the things it does not own: the file's frame rate, the origin's clock and the clock on my own wall. A startup that is shorter than the origin's own span, or longer than the whole test, is wrong whatever the hook says. And a test that says nothing moved shows first that something moves."

**Revision: the identity of the frame counters, and what the failures of the instrument and of the preconditions said (the first CI run of the pull request: GitHub macos-15, 3 CPUs, WebKit, two workers).** Four failures of the specs of this record were not about a late reading; I read the artifacts of each (`qa-events.json`, `qa-metrics.json`, `request-log.json`, the screenshot) and separate them.

*(1) Decoded is presented plus dropped (PB-VOD-012, both attempts of the merge gate).* The check compared `presented` with `decoded`: 113 against 135 (22 apart, 12 dropped) and 187 plus 12 frames before the first frame against 223 (24 apart, 21 dropped), with a bound of 10. A decoded frame is either presented or dropped: Media Playback Quality defines the total video frame count as incremented when a frame is displayed and when one is dropped (`totalVideoFrames` is "the total number of frames that would have been displayed if no frames are dropped"), Firefox's `FrameStatistics::GetTotalFrames()` is `mPresentedFrames + GetDroppedFrames()`, and METRICS.md defines the dropped ratio as dropped over decoded. So the counters agree as `presented + dropped + (frames before the first callback) = decoded`, not as `presented = decoded`, and a page that drops frames, which a starved page does, fails the second. By the first the same readings are 10 and 3 apart. I read the source of Firefox and the specification, not Chromium's or WebKit's code; their counters follow the identity in the two WebKit runs above, and the clean runs of the final series (nothing dropped) cannot tell the two comparisons apart, so for Chromium it rests on the specification and on the one run of the first series that dropped 7 frames and was 7 apart. What remains is the decoder running ahead of the presentation and a presented counter that is read from a frame callback a starved page ran late; the runner met the old bound of 10 on its boundary (10 frames), so it is now 15, half a second of 30 fps video: 1.5 times the largest residual seen and half of what a counter that doubles, repeats or restarts shows. The comparison is `framesUnaccountedFor`, used for the whole load and for the window (`measured.presented + measured.dropped` against `measured.decoded`), with the CI numbers and the clean runs of the four engines as unit cases, and the old comparison as a mutation that turns them red. METRICS.md says it.

*(2) A window the clock jumped in was a MediaSource rebuilt, not a late callback (PB-VOD-012, release run, once).* `qa.measureFrames(2)` rejected with 'the media clock jumped from 3.633 s to 4.733 s inside the measurement window'. A frame callback served late shows the same gap, so I read the run. It had one rebuffer of 2838 ms that began at media time 0 (`buffering-start` at 6897 ms with the clock at 0.000 after five seconds of play), an unrequested seek from 0 to 4.748 s that ended it (`seeking` at 9639 ms, `seeked` at 9736 ms), the origin asked again for segments 3 to 8 of both tracks (9671 to 9783 ms, after delivering 1 to 9) and counters that had restarted (23 decoded, 1 presented at the failure). That is a MediaSource torn down and rebuilt: the media element load algorithm resets the clock and the quality counters (both to 0, per the specification), the buffer was gone, and Shaka 5.3.3 resets the MediaSource of a player whose MediaSource has closed (`StreamingEngine.update_` calls `resetMediaSource(true)`; the refetch and the seek back to where the player was are what the run shows). I could not see why it closed (the browser's log was not captured) and the hook records no reset. The window is not retried: a retry would measure a second window of a player that had just stalled for 2.8 s on a clean network, and the row says 'no rebuffer'. The hook's gap rule (`MAX_FRAME_GAP_SEC`, 1 s) stays: by the gap alone a late callback cannot be told from a jump, and here the evidence was of a jump. What changed is the message: a window that cannot be measured now carries the player's account (`describePlayerTrouble`: the rebuffers with their duration and media time, the errors, the counters) after the hook's own. The same reset is the best reading of PB-VOD-007's `seeking` to 41.3 s `from: 0` at 13646 ms in the merge gate.

*(3) `qa.play()` rejected with an AbortError after `ended` (PB-VOD-011, release run, both attempts).* Both timelines are `paused`, `ended` at 12 s, `seeking` to 0 (the restart's own seek, `requested: false`), `buffering-start`, then the AbortError 0.4 s later in one attempt and 3.8 s later in the other (with three stall nudges of Shaka, 0.1 to 0.3 s, between), an `ended` at media time 0, and an element at readyState 0 with no duration; no Shaka error, no `unloaded`, no request to the origin since the end. A pending `play()` is rejected with an AbortError when the element is paused or its media is reset (the media element load algorithm), not when a seek is slow. So awaiting `seeked` or `playing` in place of the promise would have waited for events the emptied element was not going to fire in that attempt, and the failure would only have come later; the promise is where the rejection is told. It is a real behaviour of that engine on that runner, of the family of (2), and not a race of the spec, so the oracle stays (the row says `play()` restarts from the beginning, and a rejected `play()` did not). What the runs cannot say is whether Shaka's reset then restarts playback by itself (`resetMediaSource` calls `video.play()` after the reset when the element was playing): the spec ended at the rejection. So the failure now looks on for `RECOVERY_LOOKOUT_MS` (10 s, twice Shaka's `minTimeBetweenRecoveries`) and says whether a `playing` event followed, next to the element's state and the origin's log; the verdict is still the rejection, and the next failing run decides whether the row needs a second oracle ("rejected, and restarted by itself in N ms").

*(4) `waitFor('firstframe')` timed out after 15 s (PB-VOD-006, release run, once).* The first frame did not take 15 s. The timeline has five events and the last is `error` 4032 CONTENT_UNSUPPORTED_BY_BROWSER (critical, source `load`) at 5141 ms: 103 ms after `load-requested` and 20 ms after the manifest was loaded, with the manifest the only request the origin had seen. The browser told Shaka it could play none of the manifest's variants (the same page played the same manifest in the next attempt). A load that failed cannot present a first frame, so the wait could only time out, and 15 s is the library's `DEFAULT_WAIT_MS`, which PB-VOD-001 uses as well, so there is no budget of the startup to derive (the startup is not asserted at the merge tier). What was wrong was the message: its headline named the wait, and the cause was in the timeline. `waitForFirstFrame`, the precondition of every spec of this record, now puts the critical error first, with how long after the load was requested it came.

*What was asked of the library, and what could not be verified.* A wait that rejects at once when a critical error is on the timeline (it would have saved 15 s in (4)); `emptied` and Shaka's `mediasourcerecovered` on the timeline, so that a reset is an event and not an inference from the counters and the log; and a wait for the media clock that returns the page time of its reading next to the media time. None of this round was run in a browser: the rules of the round allowed none, and a starved page cannot be made here. The helpers are proven by unit tests with the readings of the runs and with synthetic late observers, and by mutations (ADR-092); whether the specs now pass on the runner is for the runner to say, and the readings of the next run are the evidence for every statement above that begins "I could not".
