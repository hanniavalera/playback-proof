# Player page and `window.__qa` decisions

Architecture decision records for the player page, the test hook and the metrics (ADR-060 to ADR-069). They are written so each one can be defended in a review: what the situation was, what was chosen, what was rejected, what it costs, and how I would argue for it. The lab-wide decisions this builds on are in [DESIGN_DECISIONS.md](../DESIGN_DECISIONS.md), in particular ADR-001 (the browser matrix) and ADR-005 (tests wait on events, with a cursor); the metric definitions are in [METRICS.md](../METRICS.md) and the hook's contract in [`src/shared/qa.ts`](../../src/shared/qa.ts).

**Status of the evidence.** The page, the hook and the metrics are unit-tested against fake elements and fake players (every claim about Shaka below was read in the code of the installed 5.3.3 and is cited by file), and then played in real engines by the smoke tool (`tools/player-smoke.ts`): Chromium 156 and WebKit 27.2 natively on macOS arm64; Chromium 156, Firefox 157 and WebKit 27.2 (Playwright's Linux build, for the experiment of ADR-067) in the pinned Playwright container on Linux arm64. Statements marked **Measured** come from those runs and name their result file under [`docs/results/player-smoke/`](../results/player-smoke/). The committed matrices (`local-darwin-arm64.json`, `docker-linux-arm64.json`) hold one run per session: a smoke, not a distribution. Where a rate matters (ADR-067) the number of runs and the upper bound on the failure rate are stated, and every timing was taken on a machine shared with other work, so a time is an order of magnitude, not a baseline.

## ADR-060: The hook records an event timeline with a cursor, and waits are on events, not on polling

**Context.** Every test, baseline, A/B run and soak observes the player through one object. A player is an event machine (`playing`, `seeking`, `buffering`, `variantchanged`...), and what a test needs to know is almost always "has X happened since I did Y". Polling the state (`expect.poll(() => qa.metrics().state)`) cannot tell "the seek finished" from "the seek has not started yet", misses anything that happens and resolves between two polls, and adds the polling interval to every wait.

**Decision.** `window.__qa` exists synchronously when the script starts and records every event the player and the element produce into an append-only timeline (`src/player/timeline.ts`): `index`, the page clock `t`, `name`, the media time and a JSON-safe `detail`, frozen once recorded. `cursor()` is the index the next event will get. `waitFor(name, { since, predicate, timeoutMs })` first scans the history from `since`, then subscribes, in one synchronous step, so an event that already happened resolves the wait and none can slip between the scan and the subscription. A test takes a cursor before an action and waits for events after it. Actions resolve on their own proof (`seek` on the `seeked` that follows its own `seeking`, `selectVariantByHeight` on the `variant-changed` it causes), `waitForMediaTime` is driven by `timeupdate` and by video frame callbacks, and every timeout rejects with the last 15 events (name, time, media time, a short detail), so a failure explains itself. The timeout is a safety net: it is never the way a wait succeeds. Listeners are called in index order even when a listener records a new event, a throwing listener cannot lose an event, a misspelt event name or a timeout of 0 or `Infinity` fails at once instead of after 20 s, and `destroy` fails the pending waits instead of leaving them to time out.

**Alternatives considered.** Polling `metrics()` (above). Playwright's auto-waiting on DOM state (the DOM does not carry media state). Exposing Shaka's own events to the test (they are not a complete account: nothing in Shaka says `seeked`, and a test would be written against Shaka's event model rather than the lab's contract). Resolving only on future events (a test that acts and then waits would race the very event it waits for: this is the bug the cursor exists to remove).

**Consequences.** The page carries a small instrumentation layer that must itself be correct, which is why it has no DOM and no Shaka in it and is tested with a fake clock. The history grows without bound during a soak, by the events of the stream: a few at startup, then only rung changes, stalls and seeks. A predicate runs in the page, so it can only look at the event.

**Defending it.** "A media test fails in two ways: it waits too short and flakes, or too long and is slow. Both come from not knowing when the player did something. The hook knows, so the test asks it, and the ask cannot race the answer because the history is searched before the subscription starts."

## ADR-061: Metrics are pure functions of the timeline, not numbers the page accumulates

**Context.** If the page keeps counters (a rebuffer count that increments, a running total of playing time), a definition lives in the order in which handlers happened to run, cannot be tested without a browser, and cannot be re-read from a failure: the counter says 3 and nobody knows which three events it counted.

**Decision.** `src/player/metrics.ts` computes every metric of [METRICS.md](../METRICS.md) from the event list and a snapshot of the media element, with no DOM and no Shaka: `startupTimes`, `bufferingEpisodes`, `rebufferMetrics`, `playingTimeMs`, `bitrateMetrics`, `errorsFromEvents`, `deriveState`, `computeMetrics`. The hook's `metrics()` only collects the snapshot (`currentTime`, the buffered ranges, `getVideoPlaybackQuality()`, the active variant, the live edge) and calls the function. A measurement describes the latest load; errors cover the page's life. The few quantities that are samples, not events (the live latency maximum, the presented-frame count, the frame window of `measureFrames`) are small classes of their own (`LatencyTracker`, `PresentedCounter`, `FrameWindow`), also pure. The unit tests feed fake timelines, so a definition is tested at its edges: a stall that never ends is reported with the duration so far and flagged, a stall that starts before the first frame is startup, a stall inside a seek is not counted, the ratio is 0 with nothing played and 1 with only a stall.

**Alternatives considered.** Counters in the handlers (above). Computing in the test harness from `events()` (every harness would implement the definitions again, slightly differently). Using Shaka's `getStats()` (it counts `stateHistory` and `bufferingTime` by its own rules, which are not the lab's, differ between versions, and cannot be asked "since the first frame, excluding seeks").

**Consequences.** `metrics()` is O(events) per call. That is fine for a test and for the HUD; a loop that polls it at high frequency over a very long soak should read it sparingly. The definitions had to be made precise enough to be code, which found two things: the seek-window rule of [METRICS.md](../METRICS.md) as first written could hide a real stall (ADR-063), and the latency formula measured something other than what the readiness limits assume (ADR-066). Both are fixed in the document in the same change.

**Defending it.** "A metric is a definition, and a definition is a function. Mine take events in and give numbers out, so when a number looks wrong I can replay the events that made it, and when someone disputes the definition I can show the test that pins it."

## ADR-062: The startup clock runs from `load-requested` to the first frame the compositor presents

**Context.** "Startup" can end at `loadedmetadata`, at `canplay`, at `playing`, or when a picture is on the screen. The first three are about the element's state, not about what a viewer sees; `playing` can fire before any frame is presented, and a black frame is not a started video.

**Decision.** `firstframe` is recorded from `requestVideoFrameCallback`, registered when the load is requested, the first time the callback fires for the load. It is the first frame handed to the compositor. Where the API is missing, the first `playing` of the load stands in, and the event says so (`detail.source`). The start of the clock is `load-requested`, recorded immediately before `player.load()`. The callback is re-armed for every frame afterwards, which also feeds `waitForMediaTime` at frame granularity (4 Hz `timeupdate` would otherwise be the finest resolution) and the presented-frame count. A load first unloads what is playing, so frames of the previous content cannot answer the callback of the next load. Page load and the download of the player library are outside the number on purpose: a player team does not control them per title ([METRICS.md](../METRICS.md)).

**Alternatives considered.** `playing` (earlier than the picture, and it moves with the autoplay policy). `loadeddata` (the data is there, nothing is shown). A canvas read-back or a screenshot (pixels, but slow, intrusive and different per engine). `performance.now()` after `play()` (starts the clock at the wrong place and ignores load).

**Consequences.** The callback is a per-frame call, 30 times a second, for as long as a load is active; it does almost nothing. With `autoplay=0` an engine that paints the first frame of a paused video reports it at load, and one that does not reports it at play: the number is then "time to a picture", which is what the definition says, but it differs per engine. **Measured** (`local-darwin-arm64.json`, `docker-linux-arm64.json`, `experiments/`): `requestVideoFrameCallback` exists in all five engine builds (Chromium on both platforms, WebKit on both, Firefox) and the stored event excerpts of the four builds that ran the scenarios show `firstframe` recorded from it, not from the `playing` fallback. Startup, `load-requested` to first frame, muted, one run per session: Chromium 121-160 ms on macOS and 42-75 ms in the container, WebKit 138-317 ms on macOS, Firefox 64-128 ms in the container. All five builds do present the first frame of a **paused** MSE video: in the 25 sessions where the page's own `play()` was refused (ADR-067, 5 per build) the element stayed paused, `state` was `ready`, exactly one frame had been presented and `firstframe` was on the timeline, so "time to a picture" exists without autoplay. `autoplay=0` itself was not run as a session of its own; a refused `play()` leaves the element in the same state (loaded, paused).

**Defending it.** "Startup is what a viewer waits for before they see something. Only the compositor knows when that is, and `requestVideoFrameCallback` is how a page asks it."

## ADR-063: Seek-caused buffering is excluded by a seek window that is closed by evidence, and decided where the stall starts

**Context.** A seek is allowed to buffer; an unprompted stall is the defect ([METRICS.md](../METRICS.md)). Shaka 5.3.3 with the default `rebufferingGoal` of 0 reports `buffering` from the element's own events (`lib/media/buffering_observer.js`: `seeking` and `waiting` start an episode; `playing`, `canplaythrough`, `seeked` or a `timeupdate` that advanced end it), so **every** seek, including one inside the buffered range, produces a short `buffering` episode, and Shaka itself starts in the buffering state, so every load has one before the first frame. The first definition ("the seek window runs from `seeking` to the first `playing` after `seeked`") has a hole: a seek inside the buffered range never takes the element out of the playable state, so no `playing` follows it, and a window that waits for one stays open and hides the next real stall.

**Decision.** The window opens at `seeking` and closes at the first of: a `playing` after `seeked`; the `buffering-end` after `seeked` (Shaka says the player has recovered); `seeked` itself when no episode is open at that moment. An episode is excluded when it **starts** before the first frame or inside a window, and counted otherwise, whatever it overlaps later: a stall that began before the seek is a stall, a stall the seek caused is the seek's cost even if it ends after `playing`. This depends on an ordering the hook guarantees by construction: the hook listens to the media element before Shaka exists (`main.ts` creates the hook first), and listeners of one target run in registration order, so `seeking` is recorded before the `buffering` Shaka reports in reaction to it; a unit test pins the order with a fake player that registers its own `seeking` listener. A stall still going on is counted with its duration so far and flagged `ongoing`; one open when the viewer unloads ends there.

**Alternatives considered.** The literal rule (the hole above; the unit test `a seek that needs no recovery does not swallow the next real stall` fails without the third closing rule). A fixed time after `seeked` (one more constant that is right on one machine). Excluding by the initiator of the seek (the element reports `seeking` for the viewer's seek, for a gap jump and for Shaka's recovery alike; the hook can mark the seeks it makes, but a person using the native controls would then count as "the player"). Counting seek-caused buffering and subtracting an expected cost (an expectation that would itself need a definition and a baseline).

**Consequences.** The window cannot tell a viewer's seek from a seek the player makes for itself: a stall that starts after a gap jump is excluded too. Such seeks are on the timeline (`detail.requested` says whether the hook made it), and catch-ups to the live edge are counted separately (`live.edgeSeeks`). A seek that never completes keeps its window open and hides the stalls after it; the `seeking` without a `seeked` is the evidence. With a non-zero `rebufferingGoal` Shaka stops using the element's events and polls the buffer instead, so "a rebuffer" would mean something else; that is the reason configuration B leaves it alone (ADR-064). **Measured** (the `actions` session in the four builds that ran it; the excerpts are stored in `local-darwin-arm64.json` and `docker-linux-arm64.json`): a viewer's seek, inside the buffer (about 3.8 s to 1 s) and outside it (2.3 s to 39 s), is recorded in every build as `seeking`, `buffering-start` (0 to 2 ms later), `seeked` (23 to 56 ms later in Chromium and Firefox, 144 and 224 ms in WebKit on macOS) and `buffering-end` (the same millisecond as `seeked`). `playing` follows in Chromium and Firefox and **does not follow in WebKit**: the case the third closing rule exists for, since a window that waited for `playing` would have stayed open for the rest of the session. No seek was counted as a rebuffer in any engine, and `rebuffer.count` is 0 in all 28 sessions of the two committed matrices.

**Defending it.** "The rule has to be right in both directions: it must not charge the player for the cost of a seek, and it must not let a seek hide a real stall. I found the second failure by writing the test for it, so the rule has a third closing condition and a test that fails without it."

## ADR-064: Configuration A is Shaka's defaults; B is three falsifiable overrides, each with a hypothesis and the condition that would reject it

**Context.** The A/B runner (phase 6) compares two player configurations on startup time and rebuffering, and the owner has to defend what is compared. A tuned configuration with no stated reason is a coin toss with extra steps; a bundle cannot say which change did what.

**Decision.** A has no override: it is the control, and it is not tuned. B is three knobs, `B_KNOBS` in [`src/player/configs.ts`](../../src/player/configs.ts), each with a hypothesis written as a prediction (what should move, under which fault profile, what it costs, what would reject it), type-checked against Shaka's own declaration of `PlayerConfiguration` so a key that does not exist in 5.3.3 is a compile error. The defaults below were read in `lib/util/player_configuration.js` and the ABR logic in `lib/abr/simple_abr_manager.js` of 5.3.3.

| Knob | Override (default) | Hypothesis, in short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `conservative-start` | `abr.useNetworkInformation: false`, `abr.defaultBandwidthEstimate: 500000` (default 1 Mbps, **replaced by `navigator.connection.downlink` wherever the browser has it**) | Until 128 KB are measured, the first rung comes from this estimate. In A it can differ per engine (Chromium has the Network Information API; Firefox and WebKit are expected not to); B pins 500 kbps everywhere, below the 360p variant (700 + 64 kbps), so B starts on the lowest rung. Expected: faster startup when the link limits it (`constrained-600`, `constrained-1500`), one extra `abr` switch, a few seconds at low resolution. No effect expected under `high-latency` alone: B saves no round trip. |
| `faster-switching` | `abr.switchInterval: 4` (default 8) | Shaka re-evaluates the rung at most once per interval, so after the link weakens the player keeps asking for the old rung until the next evaluation. Expected under `bandwidth-drop`: fewer, shorter rebuffers; cost: more rung changes on a noisy link. |
| `deeper-buffer` | `streaming.bufferingGoal: 20` (default 10) | Shaka fetches until that many seconds are buffered; that lead is what the player lives on when the link slows. Expected under `bandwidth-drop`: fewer, shorter rebuffers, startup unaffected; cost: up to twice the bytes ahead of the playhead and wasted bytes at a rung switch. No effect on live (the playhead is about 6 s behind the edge). |

Deliberately **not** in B: `streaming.rebufferingGoal`. At 0, Shaka's `buffering` is the element's own report; above 0, Shaka polls the buffer and pauses playback itself when the lead falls under 0.5 s, so a rebuffer would mean two different things in the two arms and the experiment would compare two instruments. A unit test fails if B ever sets it. `qa.info()` returns the preset letter, the exact override object, and what Shaka's defaults held at those keys (read from the player before the overrides were applied), so a result file says "switchInterval 4, default 8" without anyone looking anything up; `qa.configure()` calls are recorded too, and `configure` throws when Shaka rejects the object, so a misspelt key cannot make an experiment silently compare A with A.

**Alternatives considered.** A single "tuned" bundle with a paragraph of intent (not falsifiable, not attributable). `rebufferingGoal: 1` as the headline knob (it changes the instrument). Lowering `defaultBandwidthEstimate` alone (a no-op in Chromium, where Shaka ignores it for the network guess: found by reading `getDefaultBandwidth_`, and the reason for pairing it with `useNetworkInformation: false`). ABR restrictions or a fixed rung (that is a pin, not a tuning).

**Consequences.** A's first rung depends on the engine, which is a property of the defaults and not a bug of the page; the A/B must therefore run within one engine (it does: Chromium). Because the knobs touch different keys, a run can switch one on alone through `qa.configure` to attribute an effect. The hypotheses are predictions: a null result under `high-latency` is the expected outcome for `conservative-start`, and an A/B that shows one is a reason to look for noise, not a success. **Measured** (`vod-dash`, one session per build; `local-darwin-arm64.json`, `docker-linux-arm64.json`, `experiments/config-b-*.json`): the prediction held. Playwright's Chromium reports `navigator.connection.downlink` of 9.8 Mbps on macOS and 9.1 Mbps in the container, Shaka takes it instead of its 1 Mbps default, and A starts on **720p** and never switches in the 3 s. WebKit (macOS and Linux) and Firefox have no Network Information API (`downlink` is `null`), so A starts on the 1 Mbps default, **360p**, and switches up to 720p once Shaka has measured the first segments: 1.1 s after the start in WebKit on macOS, 12 ms in Firefox, 19 ms in WebKit on Linux (one session each). B starts on **240p** in all four builds it ran in (macOS Chromium and WebKit, container Chromium and Firefox), and `qa.info()` shows its three overrides next to the defaults they replaced. Within the 3 s window B has climbed to 720p in the container (Chromium and Firefox) and, for the VOD session, in WebKit on macOS, and has stayed on 240p in Chromium on macOS. The A/B therefore starts from two different rungs in Chromium (720p against 240p): the start rung has to be reported next to startup (`startRungHeight` is in every smoke result), or the effect of the first-rung rule is credited to the network.

**Defending it.** "I did not tune A and call it a win. B is three predictions I can state in a sentence, with the profile that should show each one and the result that would make me drop it. If the experiment says no, B loses, which is what an experiment is for."

## ADR-065: `measureFrames` counts in media time

**Context.** Effective frame rate is presented frames per second. "Per second" can be wall seconds or media seconds, and they differ exactly when something is wrong: a stall of two seconds inside a five-second wall window reads as 18 fps in wall time, though every frame that was shown was shown at 30 fps, and that is a rebuffer, which has its own metric.

**Decision.** `measureFrames(mediaSeconds)` (`FrameWindow`, `src/player/metrics.ts`) opens its window at the first frame callback and closes it when the media clock, read from the callbacks' own `mediaTime`, has advanced by the requested amount. fps is presented frames divided by the media time that **elapsed** between the first and the last callback, not by the requested value and not by a wall clock. Frames are counted from the engine's running `presentedFrames` where it has one (one callback can cover several frames when the main thread is busy; a callback counter would under-count exactly on a loaded machine), otherwise one per callback; decoded and dropped are the deltas of `getVideoPlaybackQuality()` over the same window. The promise rejects, with the timeline, when no frame arrives for `stallTimeoutMs` (default 5 s: a stall during the measurement is reported, never hidden) and when the media clock jumps (a seek inside the window makes the number meaningless).

**Alternatives considered.** Frames over wall time (the stall reads as low fps). A timer-driven sampling loop (a fixed sleep: the thing ADR-005 forbids, and it samples the main thread's mood). `getVideoPlaybackQuality().totalVideoFrames` alone (decoded, not presented: it reads full while the compositor skips).

**Consequences.** On a stalled stream `measureFrames` fails instead of returning a low number, so a performance run that stalled is a failed run, which is the intent. Where the engine has no `requestVideoFrameCallback` it reports decoded and dropped but no presented count and no fps. **Measured** (`local-darwin-arm64.json`, `docker-linux-arm64.json`): the presented count exists in all five builds. In a clean 3 s session it is 91 in Chromium (decoded 95), 89 to 91 in WebKit on macOS (decoded 95 to 96) and 90 to 92 in Firefox (decoded 89 to 91), with 0 frames dropped everywhere. The presented and decoded counts are two instruments, and they agree only roughly: over the whole `actions` session, with its pause and two seeks, decoded/presented read 272/260 in Chromium, 333/260 in WebKit on macOS and 258/281 in Firefox (and 259/403 in a second Firefox run), so after seeks one engine decodes frames it never presents and another presents more than it reports as decoded. A check may therefore compare them only within a tolerance and only over uninterrupted playback, and the whole-session `frames.presented` is not a frame-rate measurement. `measureFrames(2)` over 2 s of media time read 60 presented, 0 dropped, 2.000 s and 30 fps in Chromium, WebKit and Firefox (decoded 60, or 59 in the container): the window is what makes that number clean.

**Defending it.** "Frame rate answers 'how smooth is what was shown'. Rebuffering answers 'how long was nothing shown'. If one number tries to answer both, it answers neither, so frames are counted against the media clock and the stall shows up where stalls are measured."

## ADR-066: Live latency is the distance to the live edge, which is not `seekRange().end - currentTime`

**Context.** [METRICS.md](../METRICS.md) first defined live latency as `seekRange().end - currentTime`. Reading Shaka 5.3.3: `getSeekRangeEnd()` is `getSegmentAvailabilityEnd() - presentationDelay` for a live presentation (`lib/media/presentation_timeline.js`), and the playhead starts there (`startTime` of Infinity is clamped to it, `lib/player.js`), so on a healthy stream that formula reads about 0 and measures only how far the playhead has drifted from Shaka's own target. The readiness limits in [`src/shared/criteria.ts`](../../src/shared/criteria.ts) assume the opposite zero: a target equal to the presentation delay (6 s), a p95 limit of one segment above it and a maximum of two. With the first formula those limits could not trip until the playhead was 8 s adrift.

**Decision.** `latencyMs` is the end of the newest segment the origin has published (`presentationTimeline.getSegmentAvailabilityEnd()`, in the player's clock, corrected by the manifest's UTCTiming) minus `currentTime`, clamped at 0; a healthy stream reads about the presentation delay, which is reported next to it (`live.presentationDelayMs`) so either quantity can be read off the other (`seekRange().end - currentTime` is the latency minus the delay). The maximum counts from the first frame. Samples are taken on `timeupdate` and when a snapshot is requested. [METRICS.md](../METRICS.md) is changed in the same commit.

**Alternatives considered.** Keeping the first formula and changing the readiness limits (the number would no longer be "how far behind the live event is the viewer"). Shaka's `getLiveLatency()` (not exported in the compiled build, and based on `Date.now()`, which the lab does not trust: the service says so with UTCTiming). Both numbers as separate fields (more surface for the same information: the delay next to the latency is enough).

**Consequences.** The measurement reaches into `player.getManifest().presentationTimeline`, a structure Shaka documents as not covered by its compatibility promise; the player is pinned at 5.3.3 and the smoke asserts a live latency, so an upgrade that breaks it fails loudly (a `null` latency on a live stream is a failed check, not a silent 0). Shaka also logs a warning about that guarantee on **every** `getManifest()` call, and the first version of the hook asked at every `timeupdate`: the first real WebKit and Firefox runs filled the console with it, so the hook now reads the timeline once per load and keeps it (a unit test counts the calls). **Measured** (`live-dash` and `live-hls`, one session each per build): a healthy live session reads **6.06 to 6.40 s**, that is the 6 s presentation delay of the service's live stream plus 0.06 to 0.40 s: Chromium 6149 ms (macOS) and 6060 to 6069 ms (container), WebKit 6393 to 6399 ms on macOS and 6087 to 6133 ms on Linux (20 sessions), Firefox 6064 to 6068 ms. DASH and HLS agree within 9 ms in every build. The readiness limits of [`criteria.ts`](../../src/shared/criteria.ts) (target 6 s) are therefore on the right side of the number.

**Defending it.** "The number a live owner is paged about is how far behind the live event the viewer is. Shaka's seek range already sits six seconds behind that event by design, so I measured from the event itself, kept the delay visible next to it, and fixed the document that said otherwise instead of fixing the limits to match a wrong number."

## ADR-067: Shaka is served byte for byte, through MSE, with no request but the page's own

**Context.** The system under test is Shaka Player 5.3.3 in MSE mode, and "which bytes ran" has to be answerable. Bundling Shaka's compiled file through a bundler re-prints it (the bundle was 1.6 MB for an 867 KB file), and Chromium and macOS WebKit answer "maybe" to `canPlayType` for HLS, so a page could silently use the element's native pipeline and test something else.

**Decision.** `npm run build:player` (esbuild's JS API) copies Shaka's own `shaka-player.compiled.js` to `dist/player/shaka.js` unchanged and prints its sha256 (a unit test compares it with the file in `node_modules`), and bundles the lab's code into `player.js` (unminified, with a source map, so a stack trace reads). The page loads the two in that order. `index.html` carries a content-security-policy that allows same-origin scripts and requests only (`media-src` also needs `blob:` for MSE) and no inline script or `eval`: the page cannot reach the network on its own, and a test of the build fails if the policy loosens. `preferNativeHls` stays at Shaka's default of false, `qa.info().loadMode` reports `player.getLoadMode()` (`'media-source'` or `'src='`), and the smoke fails any session that did not play through `'media-source'`. The page is muted by default and sets `muted` before any source is attached, so that autoplay is allowed in every engine; `muted=0` is a parameter, not a default (see the Linux WebKit findings in [CODEC_MATRIX.md](../CODEC_MATRIX.md), and the measurements below).

**Alternatives considered.** One bundle with Shaka inside (re-printed bytes, a hash that proves nothing). Loading Shaka from a CDN (a network dependency and different bytes per day). Shaka's debug build (larger, slower, different timing). No policy (nothing would say that the page makes no request of its own).

**Consequences.** Two scripts instead of one: `window.__qa` appears after Shaka has been parsed, and a test waits for it with `waitForFunction`. A change of Shaka version is a change of `package.json`, and the build, its hash and the smoke say so.

**Measured: the policy and the media path** (`local-darwin-arm64.json`, `docker-linux-arm64.json`). The page boots under the policy in all five engine builds, and the `vod-dash` session of every build then tries three things from inside the page and requires the policy to have refused each: an inline script did not run, code from a string did not run (a `setTimeout` with a string, answered by a `securitypolicyviolation` event; not `new Function`, because the automation evaluates the probe with an allowance for code generation that page code does not have, and Chromium let `new Function` through, which the first version of the probe found), and a `fetch` of a `data:` URL, which is outside the page's origin, was rejected. The consoles held no message caused by the policy. Every HLS session reports `loadMode: 'media-source'` in every build, although Chromium on both platforms and WebKit on macOS answer "maybe" to `canPlayType('application/vnd.apple.mpegurl')` (Firefox and WebKit on Linux answer an empty string): the page does not take the element's native pipeline anywhere, which is what `preferNativeHls: false` is for.

**Measured: unmuted autoplay is not accepted by any engine on its own, and the usual run hides that** (`experiments/unmuted-*.json`, `experiments/muted-hold2000-*.json`). With the page left alone for 2 s after navigation (`--hold-ms 2000`: no evaluate of the tool has run yet), the page's own unmuted `play()` was refused with `NotAllowedError` in **25 of 25** sessions, 5 in each of Chromium on macOS, Chromium in the container, WebKit on macOS, WebKit on Linux and Firefox; the same hold with the page muted passed **20 of 20** (4 builds, 5 each: the Linux WebKit control was not repeated). Without the hold, unmuted sessions mostly pass, because the tool's own first evaluate is run as a user gesture (Playwright 1.64.1 sends Chromium's evaluations with `userGesture: true` and WebKit's with `emulateUserGesture: true`; its Firefox call carries no such flag and Firefox shows the same dependence all the same, so the mechanism there is not established) and it normally lands before the page calls `play()`: Firefox 7 of 7, WebKit on macOS 7 of 7, Chromium in the container 7 of 7, WebKit on Linux 50 of 50 (`vod-dash` 20, `vod-hls` 20, `live-dash` 10), Chromium on macOS **26 of 27**. The one refusal was the first session of its run, which is the race `--hold-ms` makes deterministic; it failed at the stage `playback`, with the timeline in the error message (its signature then was `0 via play`, which is why the hook now names the error: ADR-068). So: muted stays the default because it needs no gesture in any engine, and a test that wants sound starts playback with `qa.play()` from the test (an evaluate, so with a gesture) instead of relying on the page's autoplay. The refusal is visible as an error event with `source: 'play'` and `codeName: 'NotAllowedError'` (see ADR-068), and as a session that stays `ready` and paused with its first frame presented.

**Measured: the Linux WebKit experiment** (`experiments/linux-webkit-*.json`; WebKit 27.2 in `mcr.microsoft.com/playwright:v1.64.1-noble`, arm64, `--cpus=4`, one container at a time, a fresh browser process per run, `--no-capabilities` because the committed codec matrix marks this engine unsupported). The probe's own page passed 9 of 20 attempts there with a muted element (ADR-031 and ADR-032 in [codecs.md](codecs.md)). This page, with Shaka adding all SourceBuffers before fetching, passed **80 of 80** runs: `vod-dash` 20 of 20 muted and 20 of 20 unmuted, `vod-hls` 20 of 20 and 20 of 20, and then `live-dash` 10 of 10 and 10 of 10. A pass is 3 s of media time through `waitForMediaTime`, `firstframe` seen, zero error events (and `loadMode: 'media-source'`). There was no failure, so there is no failure signature to record: no `NotSupportedError`, no Shaka error code, no recovered MediaSource. With 0 failures in 80 runs the **95 % upper bound on this page's failure rate is 3.7 %** (exact one-sided Clopper-Pearson; the rule of three gives 3.75 %); per cell it is 13.9 % (rule of three 15 %) for 20 runs and 25.9 % (30 %) for 10. How far that goes: one image, one architecture (arm64 in Docker's Linux VM), one WebKit build, one stream and 3 s of playback per run, on a machine shared with other work; it does not contradict the probe's page, it shows that a player which drives MSE the way Shaka does did not reproduce the intermittency in 80 runs. Nothing in the lab's defaults or its codec documents was changed on this evidence; the decision on running WebKit on Linux is the orchestrator's.

**Defending it.** "The page runs the exact bytes npm published and prints their hash. It cannot download anything, and a session that did not play through MSE fails the smoke instead of passing for something it was not."

## ADR-068: Errors never crash the page, and none is swallowed

**Context.** A test must be able to assert "there were no errors", and a failing run must say what failed. Shaka reports errors in four different ways (an `error` event, a rejected `load()`, no event at all after it silently resets the MediaSource, and a retry below the application that the app never sees), and the page itself can throw.

**Decision.** Everything becomes an `error` event on the same timeline, with `code`, `codeName` (looked up in Shaka's enums, because the compiled build's message is only "Shaka Error 1001"), `category`, `severity`, a short digest of `error.data` (URIs and status codes; binary bodies and long strings are cut) and a `source`: `shaka` (the event), `load` (a rejected `load()`, once even if Shaka also dispatched the same error), `play` (a rejected `play()`, for example an autoplay refusal), `boot`, `params` (a query parameter the page cannot use is ignored and reported, so `muted=yes` cannot silently change what is measured), `window.onerror` and `unhandledrejection` (code 0). Shaka's `mediasourcerecovered` is recorded as a recoverable error (`MEDIA_SOURCE_RECOVERED`), because after some media errors Shaka resets the MediaSource and raises no error at all: without that line a run in the engine with intermittent MSE failures would pass while having failed and recovered. A critical error (severity 2) puts `state` in `error`; a recoverable one does not; the page's own errors have severity 1. The page never rethrows: its boot sequence ends in a `catch` whose only purpose is that the failure is already on the timeline.

**Alternatives considered.** Recording only Shaka's `error` events (loses the four other channels above). Treating a recovered MediaSource as a non-event (hides the one engine behaviour the lab most needs to see). Letting an exception end the page (the test would read nothing).

**Consequences.** A smoke or a gate that demands zero errors also fails on a recovered MediaSource, which is the stricter, intended reading. A retry that Shaka's networking layer absorbs (a single 404 with the default retry policy, which is what the service's `transient-404` profile assumes) should leave **no** error event; the request log is where it shows, which is why the recovery scenario of the smoke reads both channels. **Measured** (`recovery`, one session per build that ran it: Chromium on macOS and in the container, WebKit on macOS, Firefox): the service's request log shows video segment 3 answered **404 once** by the rule that was set (`f1`) and the next request for that segment served (200); **no `error` event reached the hook** in any build, `rebuffer.count` was 0 and playback went on past the fault to 8 s of media time. In WebKit the retry went to the 360p rendition and ABR moved up afterwards. The browser's console reports the injected 404 as an error line of its own (`Failed to load resource`); the smoke keeps it in `consoleErrors` and does not count it. A refused `play()` is recorded with `source: 'play'` and, since the first unmuted run, with `codeName: 'NotAllowedError'`, the thrown error's own name: the first version recorded code 0 and left the name inside the message, so the smoke's failure signature read `0 via play`, which says nothing (any thrown error that is not a plain `Error` is named the same way; a plain `Error` has no `codeName`).

**Defending it.** "I would rather have an error event too many than a green run that hid one. A test that wants tolerance can filter by severity and source; it cannot filter out something that was never recorded."

## ADR-069: What the hook deliberately does not measure

**Context.** A hook that claims to measure everything measures nothing reliably. Each limit below is a decision, not an omission.

**Decision.** The hook does not measure: **picture or sound quality** (it counts presented frames, it does not look at them); **audio and video sync**; **the segment-level network** (what was requested, from where, how often is the service's request log, a second channel that does not depend on the page); **the viewer's real experience** (these are localhost laboratory numbers on synthetic content; see [METRICS.md](../METRICS.md), "What the numbers do not claim"); **percentiles of live latency** (it exposes the current and the maximum; the readiness harness takes p95 over its own samples of `metrics()`); **which seeks were the viewer's** (ADR-063: the element does not say, so the hook marks only the seeks it makes); **DRM** (the clear-key stream is a separate capability, and the page does not select a key system itself); **text tracks, ads, casting, Shaka's UI**. It also does not change playback: it never enables Shaka's live-sync or its buffering policy on its own, and its two actions that touch playback (`seek`, `selectVariantByHeight`) are the ones the viewer's controls would perform.

**Alternatives considered.** Adding every number Shaka exposes (`getStats()` has many): each would need a definition, a test and a baseline, and the ones that look free (`bufferingTime`) disagree with the lab's definitions.

**Consequences.** A question outside this list needs another instrument, not a bigger hook. The list is also the answer to "what would you add next": percentiles in the page if the readiness harness cannot sample, and an initiator for seeks if a player of this lab starts making seeks of its own.

**Defending it.** "Knowing what an instrument cannot see is part of using it. The hook measures what the contract promises, tests it at the edges, and says out loud where it stops."
