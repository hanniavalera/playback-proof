# Response delivery decisions (ADR-050 to ADR-054)

Architecture decision records for how the test data service writes a response body to a socket under fault injection: [`src/server/throttle.ts`](../../src/server/throttle.ts) (the shared token bucket) and [`src/server/deliver.ts`](../../src/server/deliver.ts) (latency, throttle, stall, abort, back-pressure and disconnects). They are written so each one can be defended in a review: what the situation was, what was chosen, what was rejected, what it costs, and how I would argue for it. They build on ADR-002 ([DESIGN_DECISIONS.md](../DESIGN_DECISIONS.md)) and on the semantics of the faults in [CONTRACTS.md](../CONTRACTS.md) (section 5).

**Where the numbers come from.** Every figure below is printed by the tests themselves (`console.info` lines starting `[throttle]` and `[delivery]` in [`tests/unit/server/throttle.test.ts`](../../tests/unit/server/throttle.test.ts) and [`tests/unit/server/deliver.test.ts`](../../tests/unit/server/deliver.test.ts)) and is the minimum and maximum over five consecutive runs of `npx vitest run tests/unit/server` at commit `01c532c`. Machine: Apple M2 Pro (12 cores, shared with other work), macOS, Node 22.11.0, Vitest 5.1.0, real TCP on `127.0.0.1`, client and server in the same Node process, so the client's clock and the server's clock are the same clock. The client is Node's `http` client, not a browser. Nothing here was measured on Linux or in Docker. The tests assert the contract's tolerances (for example +-10 % for the rate), not these exact numbers.

## ADR-050: The throttle is a token bucket in the service that paces the writes of the body

**Context.** ADR-002 put fault injection in the service, below the browser. For bandwidth that means the service has to make a response body arrive at a given rate, and that rate is what the player measures: Shaka estimates bandwidth from the bytes and the time of each segment download, so the number the lab quotes must be the rate a client really observes. The contract (CONTRACTS section 5) fixes the tolerance: measured goodput within +-10 % of `kbps` for transfers of at least 500 KB.

**Decision.** A token bucket in the Node process. Tokens are body bytes, the bucket refills at `kbps * 1000 / 8` bytes per second, and `deliver()` writes a piece of the body only after the bucket has granted it. Only the body is limited (the contract says headers are excluded), and the head is flushed first and on its own, so a client sees the status while the body is paced. The bucket is arithmetic over a clock that a test can inject; it knows nothing about sessions, rules or requests (the service core creates one bucket per rule and session and puts it in the plan of each response).

Measured goodput of a real client against the configured rate, on 512,000-byte bodies (`steady` is the bytes after the first read over the time from the first read to the last; `with burst` counts the first read too, which carries the 16 KB burst allowance):

| configured | steady                 | ratio       | with burst             | ratio       | duration  |
| ---------- | ---------------------- | ----------- | ---------------------- | ----------- | --------- |
| 800 kbps   | 799.7 to 799.9 kbps    | 1.000       | 826.2 to 826.4 kbps    | 1.033       | 4.96 s    |
| 1500 kbps  | 1498.8 to 1500.0 kbps  | 0.999-1.000 | 1548.4 to 1549.5 kbps  | 1.032-1.033 | 2.64 s    |
| 3000 kbps  | 2992.7 to 2998.2 kbps  | 0.998-0.999 | 3091.6 to 3097.3 kbps  | 1.031-1.032 | 1.32 s    |

**Alternatives considered.** OS shaping (`tc` and netem on Linux, `pfctl` with `dnctl` on macOS): needs root, differs per OS, is not available on the macOS CI runners or inside the Docker VM the lab uses, and shapes an interface, not a session (ADR-002). CDP `Network.emulateNetworkConditions`: Chromium only (ADR-002). A shaping proxy: a second process and a second hop to start, configure and trust, and it would need this same pacing logic inside it. A throttling stream from a package: a dependency, and every response would get its own rate (see ADR-051). A bucket topped up once per second: it turns a rate into one-second bursts, which a bandwidth estimator reads as a different rate (see ADR-052).

**Consequences.** The condition is the same in every engine, on every OS, with no privileges, and it is observable where it is applied (the request log). The price is that it limits what the service hands to the kernel, which is not everything a network does (ADR-054), and that it lives on the event loop of the service, so a blocked loop is its hazard (ADR-052 shows what happens and how it is handled). The 3.1 to 3.3 % in the "with burst" columns is the 16 KB burst allowance: 16,384 of 512,000 bytes is 3.2 %. It is by design and shrinks with the size of the transfer; it matters for short responses (a 30 KB response at 800 kbps finishes about 160 ms earlier than size over rate says).

**Defending it.** "The lab needs the same bandwidth cap in three engines, on a laptop and in CI, without root. Pacing the writes in the service is the one place that gives all of that, and the number I quote is what a client measured, not what the code intended: 0.998 to 1.000 of the configured rate at three rates over five runs, against a contract of +-10 %."

## ADR-051: One bucket is one link: shared by concurrent responses, served round robin, the slowest bucket wins

**Context.** A player fetches audio and video at the same time, and on a real connection they compete for one pipe. If each response had its own cap, a session limited to 1500 kbps would see 3000 kbps the moment two requests overlapped, and the ABR logic would be tested against a link nobody has. The contract: one bucket per rule and session shared by the concurrent responses; a response matched by several throttle rules is limited by all of them.

**Decision.** Consumers that cannot be served queue up. The head of the queue is served when the bucket holds a slice of tokens; a consumer that has just been served asks again and goes to the back of the queue, so n busy consumers take turns one grant at a time (a grant is at most 16 KB and usually one 15 ms slice): round robin, chunk by chunk. A response limited by several buckets joins all of them (`createPacer`), asks them slowest first and uses the smallest grant; what an earlier bucket granted beyond that is handed back, so a contended fast bucket does not make a slow one pay for bytes that could not be sent. Asking slowest first matters: the slow bucket is the one the response really waits for, and the fast one is then held only for the instant between two grants. A consumer that leaves (its client disconnects) is removed from the queue at once.

Measured (five runs, 1500 kbps unless noted):

- Two downloads of 250,000 bytes on one bucket: each gets 49.3 to 50.7 % of the rate, together 1498.2 to 1499.8 kbps (asserted: each within +-20 % of half, the sum within +-10 %). Four consumers on one bucket get a quarter each (fake clock).
- Audio (60,000 bytes) next to video (400,000 bytes) on one bucket: the audio finished after 645 to 668 ms. Alone it would take 320 ms; queued behind the whole video it would take 2453 ms. By then the video had moved 75.7 to 79.7 KB, which is the audio's 60 KB plus the 16 KB burst that went to whoever asked first.
- One response behind 1500 and 6000 kbps, in either order: 1495.8 to 1499.7 kbps (the slower one).
- Two responses on separate 800 and 3000 kbps buckets at the same time: 799.0 to 799.5 and 2992.6 to 2998.7 kbps (no interference).
- After one of two clients left a 1500 kbps bucket, the one that stayed ran at 1499.4 to 1514.0 kbps (it gets the whole link).

**Alternatives considered.** Whoever's timer fires first (each response sleeps on its own timer): the order is decided by timer jitter, which is not a model of anything. Strict first come, first served by response: simple, but a short audio response waits for the whole video ahead of it (2453 ms against 656 ms above), which is the opposite of how TCP flows share a link. Weighted fair queuing by bytes: it is what TCP congestion control approximates, but with grants of equal size round robin is the same thing for a fraction of the code. A bucket per response: wrong by construction (see Context). Asking the buckets fastest first: it holds the fast bucket's tokens while waiting for the slow one and takes them from the other consumers of the fast bucket.

**Consequences.** The rate of a response depends on what else the session is downloading, which is what makes ABR experiments valid, and means a test with concurrent requests must expect the shared rate, not the configured one. The first 16 KB (the burst allowance) goes to whoever asks first, so shares are equal to within one grant. Shares are equal per grant, not per round trip: TCP gives a flow with a shorter round trip more of a link, and this does not (ADR-054).

**Defending it.** "A link is shared, so the bucket is shared, and fairness is a property I can measure instead of hoping for: two downloads got 49.3 to 50.7 % each, and a 60 KB audio segment is not stuck behind a 400 KB video segment. A bucket per response would be easier to write and would answer a question nobody asked."

## ADR-052: Smooth pacing: 15 ms slices of at least one segment, a one-chunk burst, timers only while someone waits, and credit that is owed is paid out

**Context.** A rate that is right on average and delivered in bursts is a different condition from a steady one: a player's throughput estimator sees chunk arrival times. The brief asks for chunks of at most 16 KB at slices of 10 to 20 ms, and no timer while nothing is waiting (the process must be able to exit).

**Decision.**

- The largest grant, and the most credit a bucket banks while nobody is asking, is one chunk: 16 KB (`CHUNK_BYTES`), the contract's burst allowance.
- A grant is made when a slice of tokens is there (or everything a consumer still wants, if that is less). The slice is 15 ms of the rate, the middle of the target: 1500 bytes at 800 kbps, 2812 at 1500, 5625 at 3000.
- A grant is never smaller than 1460 bytes, one full-size Ethernet segment: below about 780 kbps the slice therefore grows (117 ms at 100 kbps), because a smaller write is only a fragment. Above about 8.7 Mbps the slice is capped by the chunk and shrinks below 15 ms.
- One `setTimeout` per bucket, armed only while the queue is not empty, for the moment the head of the queue can be served. Node can fire a timer a fraction of a millisecond early; the arithmetic reads the clock, not the timer, so an early wake just sleeps again. An idle bucket has no timer: a fake-timer test counts the pending timers (0 when idle, 1 while someone waits, 0 after), and a child process that used and left a bucket exits by itself (152 to 163 ms, including loading TypeScript).
- Time that accrues while a consumer is waiting is owed to it and is not discarded; only time that accrues while nobody is asking is capped at one chunk. So a late timer cannot lower the long-run rate, and a consumer that is merely not asking (back-pressure, a stall) does not bank credit. Credit still owed when the last consumer leaves is forgotten, so it cannot become a bonus burst for the next one.
- The clock is injectable, so the arithmetic is tested without timers or sockets (`tryTake`, `msUntilTake`), and the queue is tested with fake timers.

Measured at 1500 kbps over 400,000 bytes: 123 to 125 reads, the gap between reads has a median of 16.6 to 17.2 ms, a 95th percentile of 17.3 to 17.7 ms and a maximum of 17.5 to 21.6 ms (asserted: below 150 ms). The first read is the burst, 16,384 bytes (16,332 in two runs: the loopback interface has an MTU of 16,384, so a segment carries at most 16,332 bytes of payload and the 16 KB write is split), and no read is larger than that.

With the event loop blocked for 80 ms in every 200 ms (a busy host, a long garbage collection) a 3000 kbps transfer ran at 2993.1 to 2997.1 kbps, 0.998 to 0.999 of the rate. As a mutation check I changed the bucket to cap its credit also while a consumer waits (in one run): the same test gave 2481.2 kbps, 0.827. That 17 % is the reason for the rule above, and it is invisible on an idle machine: the end-to-end accuracy tests do not notice that change; only a test that makes the timers late does, so that test exists.

**Alternatives considered.** A refill once per second (bursts of a second's worth). A `setInterval` ticker (a timer that never stops: it keeps the process alive, wakes when nobody is waiting and drifts). A timer per byte or per packet, or a `setImmediate` spin (CPU for no gain in smoothness). Capping the credit always at one chunk (simpler; measured above to lose a sixth of the rate when timers are late). A virtual-time formulation (GCRA): equivalent to a token bucket and with the same question about late wake-ups.

**Consequences.** About 66 timer wake-ups per second per busy bucket between 780 kbps and 8.7 Mbps, which is negligible. At very low rates the slice is coarse (one segment per round trip of the queue) and at very high rates the 1 ms timer floor is the limit (16 KB per millisecond is about 131 Mbps). After a long stall of the loop the owed credit is paid out as a burst of chunks at once: the long-run rate is kept, the one-chunk burst bound is not, and I preferred the rate (the longest silence the client saw in the blocked-loop test was 96 to 97 ms: the 80 ms stall plus a slice).

**Defending it.** "The accuracy I quote has to survive a busy machine, because that is where CI runs. The arithmetic follows the clock, the timer only decides when to look, and the credit that was earned while somebody waited is never thrown away. I proved that by breaking it: with the credit capped, the same test lost 17 % of the rate."

## ADR-053: Back-pressure, flush before cut, disconnects, and a promise that never rejects

**Context.** `deliver()` is the only code that touches the socket, and the service runs for hours across many sessions: a reader that stops, a reader that leaves, and a write to a destroyed response are normal events, not exceptions, and a leaked timer, bucket waiter or listener would be a defect in every later measurement.

**Decision.**

- **Back-pressure.** The body is written in pieces of at most 16 KB; when `res.write` returns `false` nothing more is written until `drain`. Measured with a 20 MB body and a reader that stopped for 400 ms: the response never held more than 65,536 bytes (its high-water mark) in all five runs, while the kernel took 0.75 to 1.06 MB meanwhile (an earlier development run: 2.00 MB; it depends on the OS and is only reported). As a mutation check, ignoring `drain` queued all 20,971,520 bytes in memory; the test fails on that.
- **First byte and bytes are counted where they happen.** The head is flushed alone, with the write callback of an empty `res.write`, and `ttfbMs` is taken in that callback, not when `writeHead` is called: with the socket held corked for 150 ms, `ttfbMs` was 151.1 to 152.6 ms and the client saw the head after 152.5 to 153.6 ms. `bytesSent` is counted in the write callbacks, so it is what the operating system accepted.
- **Abort: flush, then destroy.** The pieces before the cut are flushed (the callbacks have fired), then `socket.destroy()`. The test client receives exactly 0, 1, 1234, 16384 and 70000 bytes, then a reset, with `Content-Length` still the full size. As a mutation check, destroying straight after `write()` delivered 0 of 1 requested bytes: the bytes were still in the process.
- **Stall and latency are lower bounds.** The timer is re-armed until the clock says the delay has really passed. A 300 ms stall was seen by the client as 300.6 to 303.3 ms of silence at the requested byte (inside the body and right after the head); the first byte of a 50 ms and a 250 ms latency arrived 0.9 to 5.0 ms late (`ttfbMs` 52.0 to 53.3 and 250.3 to 253.0), never early.
- **Disconnects.** The signal is the `close` event of the response before it has finished (on the request, Node 16 and later emit `close` as soon as the request has been read, which a handler may have done and which says nothing about the client). On a disconnect: every sleep is cancelled, the response leaves every bucket (a wait in the queue is cancelled at once), and the promise settles with `aborted: true` and `reason: 'client-closed'`. A response at the back of a queue of six on a 100 kbps bucket settled 0.3 to 0.6 ms after its client left; removing the cancellation made that test fail (it allows 100 ms; a round of a queue of six 117 ms slices is about 700 ms, which is when a response that only noticed at its turn would settle). With 50 clients leaving at random points (a seeded generator, half with a reset, half with a FIN) of throttled, delayed, stalled and combined responses: all 50 settled `client-closed`, `activeDeliveries()` was 0, the bucket had no waiter, no consumer and no timer, the next throttled request on the same server and bucket succeeded, no `uncaughtException` or `unhandledRejection` was seen, and `server.close()` took 0.1 to 0.4 ms.
- **A client that leaves with everything is not an abort.** A `Connection: close` client hangs up as soon as `Content-Length` is satisfied, possibly before `end()` has completed; if every byte was flushed, the outcome is a normal one.
- **Never rejects.** Every error becomes an outcome (`reason: 'error'` with an `error` text for an invalid status or header value); a hook that throws is reported in `error` and does not stop the response, because a throw inside a stream callback would be an uncaught exception; the `error` listener stays on the response for its whole life. `Content-Length` is always the real size, a `Content-Length` or `Transfer-Encoding` of the caller is replaced (in any letter case), the response is never chunked, and no `Connection` header is added.
- **HEAD.** Same headers, `Content-Length` of the would-be body, no body; only the latency of the plan applies.

**Alternatives considered.** `stream.pipeline` from a `Readable`: it handles destroy and back-pressure, but the flush barriers for stall and abort and the shared pacing need explicit control anyway, so it would add an object per response and hide the points that matter. Listening on the socket for `close`: on a keep-alive connection every request would add a listener that has to be removed; the per-request response object takes its listeners with it. A rejecting promise: every caller needs a `try`, and one forgotten `await` is an unhandled rejection that ends the service.

**Consequences.** `bytesSent` is "accepted by the operating system", so after a cut or a disconnect it can be larger than what the client read. A response that cannot be written (an invalid header) gets a dropped connection and an `error` outcome, not a 500: the service validates rules before it gets here. The abort fault with `afterBytes` at or beyond the body size sends the whole body and then destroys the socket, so a client with a keep-alive connection sees a complete response followed by a closed connection.

**Defending it.** "The failure modes of a socket are the ones I tested on purpose: a reader that stops (queue stays at the high-water mark), a reader that leaves at 50 random moments (nothing is left), a cut (exactly N bytes arrive). Each rule above exists because I broke it in the code and watched a test fail."

## ADR-054: What the throttle does not model, and the error that remains

**Context.** A cap on goodput is a model of a link, and the owner of this lab will be asked what it is not. The limits have to be written down next to the numbers, or the numbers will be read as more than they are.

**Decision and limits.**

- **Round trips.** The throttle is a rate. It has no round-trip time and no slow start: a `latency` rule delays the first byte of a response once, a real link adds a round trip to every request and ramps a connection up over several. Short responses therefore arrive faster here than on a real link of the same nominal rate.
- **Loss, retransmission and jitter inside a transfer** are not modelled. A rate that changes over time is a sequence of rules (`afterMs`, `forMs`), not a continuous process.
- **Headers and framing** are not counted: the rate is the goodput of the body, as the contract says.
- **Connections.** The bucket is shared by all the responses of a session, over however many connections the browser opens, like a bottleneck link. Per-connection effects (and HTTP/2, which the service does not speak) are not modelled.
- **Loopback buffering.** The throttle controls the rate at which bytes enter the kernel. With a client that reads promptly nothing queues: writes are at most 16 KB, Nagle's algorithm is off (`setNoDelay(true)`; Node's server already does that on 22.11, and it is set explicitly because the pacing depends on it), and the client's reads follow the writes (a 17 ms gap between reads at 1500 kbps). The error that remains: the first 16 KB go out at once (3.1 to 3.3 % of a 512,000-byte transfer in the "with burst" figure, none in the steady one); timer jitter is a few milliseconds per slice and averages out (the steady error was at most 0.3 % over 5 runs and three rates); and if the client reads more slowly than the throttle, the kernel's send and receive buffers take data ahead of it (0.75 to 1.06 MB on this macOS host before the socket pushed back) and it later reads that at loopback speed. A paused media element that stops reading could therefore receive up to that much in a burst; at the rates the lab throttles to this is far from what a browser leaves unread, but I did not measure it with a browser (none is used in this phase) and the buffer sizes on Linux differ and were not measured.
- **What was measured with what.** Node's `http` client in the same process, a monotonic clock, one host (Apple M2 Pro, macOS). Whether Chromium, Firefox and WebKit see the same goodput is for the browser suites to confirm; the numbers above are the evidence for the service, not for the browsers.

**Alternatives considered.** Modelling round trips and slow start (it means writing a TCP model, and its parameters would be guesses with no measurement behind them). Lowering the kernel send buffer (`SO_SNDBUF`) to cut the buffering error: Node does not expose it for TCP sockets, and the portable options are coarse. Counting header bytes in the bucket: it contradicts the contract and the difference is under 1 % for a segment.

**Consequences.** The lab can honestly claim "the response body arrives at `kbps`, within a measured 0.3 % in steady state, shared fairly among the session's responses, smoothly (gaps of 17 ms at 1500 kbps)". It cannot claim "this is what a 4G link does". For the questions the lab asks (does the ABR step down at 800 kbps, how long does it take to recover, does the buffer run dry) goodput is the controlled variable; for loss or radio effects it is not, and the strategy does not claim it.

**Defending it.** "I can say what the throttle is, with numbers, and I can say what it is not. The honest answer to 'does this simulate a mobile network?' is no: it holds a cap on goodput that I measured to a fraction of a percent, and it is the same cap in every browser, which is what a cross-browser comparison needs."
