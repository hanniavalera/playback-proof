# The live-event readiness decisions (ADR-150 to ADR-159)

Architecture decision records for the readiness checks and the soak: the library [`src/lab/readiness`](../../src/lab/readiness) (the verdict model, the evaluators, the check procedures, the soak harness and its evaluators, the result files and the go/no-go sheet), the two commands [`live/readiness.ts`](../../live/readiness.ts) (`npm run readiness`) and [`live/soak.ts`](../../live/soak.ts) (`npm run soak`), and the Playwright spec [`tests/live/readiness.spec.ts`](../../tests/live/readiness.spec.ts) (PB-RDY-001 to PB-RDY-005). The criteria they judge against are `LIVE_READINESS` and `LIVE_LIMITS` in [`src/shared/criteria.ts`](../../src/shared/criteria.ts), rendered in [TEST_STRATEGY.md](../../TEST_STRATEGY.md) section 8; the metrics are defined in [METRICS.md](../METRICS.md); the live origin simulation is in [CONTRACTS.md](../CONTRACTS.md) sections 3 and 5. They stand on the support library ([ADR-070 to ADR-079](e2e.md)), on what the live and recovery specs found ([ADR-098 to ADR-100](e2e-live.md), [ADR-101 to ADR-103](e2e-rec.md)) and on the measurement library of the performance harness ([ADR-120 to ADR-129](perf.md)). They are written so each one can be defended in a review: what the situation was, what was chosen, what was rejected, what it costs, and how I would argue for it.

**Where the numbers come from.** Phase 7 has two parts, and these records were written at the end of the first. Part 1 built the checks, the soak and their evaluators and **measured nothing in a browser**: the unit tests run the evaluators over synthetic series (a leak, a drift, a hung player, an outage that leaves a step), the procedures over a scripted origin and player on a virtual clock, and the command line's adapters and commands over a fake of the part of Playwright they use (ADR-150), and every number of the policy is still the initial one of Phase 1 (`assumption` or `arithmetic` in `criteria.ts`). The only measured figures quoted below are the ones that earlier phases measured and committed, each attributed to the record that holds it (on one Apple M2 Pro, with other work running, in localhost and synthetic conditions). Part 2 runs the checks and the soaks in every engine, validates every limit against what it measures and revises the ones that are wrong with the evidence; ADR-159 says how, and is where the results go.

**What a verdict means, in one paragraph.** A readiness verdict is a number, a limit and a sentence. `GO` and `NO-GO` are statements about the build. `INCONCLUSIVE` is a statement about the run (too few samples, a soak shorter than the policy asks for, an injected fault that never reached the player) and is never read as a pass. The sheet is about the build as it ships, configuration A (Shaka's own defaults); a candidate configuration is evaluated next to it, as evidence for a decision, and never makes the build `GO`.

## ADR-150: The checks are written once, over an interface of the player, the service and a fresh browser context

**Context.** The brief asked for two entry points: Playwright specs (`@release @live`, ids PB-RDY-001 to PB-RDY-004, run in the three engines through the support library) and a command, `npm run readiness`, that prints the go/no-go sheet and writes a result file per environment and engine. The sheet needs structured results (the samples, the value, the limit, the verdict of every line, for the engines of a run side by side with a candidate configuration), and a spec needs the fixtures that make a failure explain itself (the session deleted after the test, the diagnostics attached, the documented-gap skips). The two must not be able to disagree about what a check is.

**Decision.** The procedures of the four checks live in [`checks.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/checks.ts) and are written against `ReadinessWorld` ([`world.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/world.ts)): a player (`open`, `openIdle`, `loadAndPlay`, `metrics`, `info`, `cursor`, `events`, `waitFor`, `waitForMediaTime`, `configure`), the part of the service client they use, and `newRun`, which gives a fresh session and a player on a **fresh browser context** (a viewer who opens the page, as in ADR-120). The support library's `PlayerHandle` has every one of these members with these signatures except `openIdle` and `loadAndPlay` (a handle always opens the page with a manifest; ADR-153 says why a candidate needs a page that has none), so the spec wraps it (`specPlayer` in [`tests/live/support.ts`](../../tests/live/support.ts) delegates the members the handle has and takes the two it lacks from [`page.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/page.ts), where the command's player has them too; `specWorld` builds a world out of the fixtures `newSession` and `player`, so that a failing check still carries its diagnostics); the command passes the performance harness's `QaPage` through a thin adapter ([`page.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/page.ts)); the unit tests pass a scripted origin and player on a virtual clock ([`fake-world.ts`](../../tests/unit/readiness/fake-world.ts)). The checks return structured results (`CheckResult`: the evaluations, the samples, what ended the check, the configuration the page said it had applied); the spec asserts on the evaluations and attaches the whole result as JSON, the command renders it. The command drives the browser through the Playwright **library** API (`launchProfile`, one process per engine, a fresh context per run), not through the runner.

**Alternatives considered.** The command running `playwright test tests/live/readiness.spec.ts` and reading the JSON reporter's output (it would need no adapter, but the structured series would have to travel through annotations and attachments and be parsed out again; the runner's webServer, its reporters and the flake history would be started for a measurement that is not a test run; and a candidate configuration or a comparison between two configurations has no place in a test result). Two implementations, one in the spec and one in the command (they would drift, and the unit tests would test one of them). Putting the checks in the spec and the evaluators in the library (the spec would then hold the sequence that decides whether a check can fail for the wrong reason, in the one place a unit test cannot reach).

**Consequences.** There is one place to read what PB-RDY-003 does to a session, and the procedures are covered by unit tests that run in milliseconds ([`checks.test.ts`](../../tests/unit/readiness/checks.test.ts)). The adapters ([`page.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/page.ts), [`playwright-world.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/playwright-world.ts), [`soak-driver.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/soak-driver.ts)) and the two commands ([`commands.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/commands.ts), to which `live/readiness.ts` and `live/soak.ts` hand the real service and the real browsers) are the part that needs a real browser, and are deliberately thin. They are run by unit tests over a **fake of the part of Playwright they use** ([`fake-playwright.ts`](../../tests/unit/readiness/fake-playwright.ts)): a page function is evaluated from its source text in a scope that has the page's globals and nothing of the harness's (a variable it closes over, a Node global, a helper the transformer left in it are errors, as in the browser), arguments and results cross by structured clone, the DevTools session answers as Chromium does, and the clock is virtual, so a five-minute soak runs in milliseconds ([`playwright-adapters.test.ts`](../../tests/unit/readiness/playwright-adapters.test.ts), [`playwright-soak-driver.test.ts`](../../tests/unit/readiness/playwright-soak-driver.test.ts), [`commands.test.ts`](../../tests/unit/readiness/commands.test.ts)). What that proves is the plumbing: the order of the calls, what crosses the boundary, what is closed and deleted, the exit codes, and that the adapters add nothing to and lose nothing from what the checks measure (the same four checks give the same verdicts and numbers through the fake page as against the fake world directly). What it cannot prove is the browser's side: the real hook, the real service, the real DevTools protocol, the page's content security policy and the player itself. Those are part 1's second half and ADR-159 lists them. In the spec a session is deleted when the test ends and not when its run closes, so the diagnostics of a failed check can read every session it made; in the command the session is deleted when the run closes. A check that takes several runs makes several players, and a failure attaches the documents of each (`qa-events-2.json`, and so on, as in section 7.2 of the contract).

**Defending it.** "The sequence of a check is the thing that can make it pass for the wrong reason, so it is the thing I wrote once and tested without a browser. The spec and the command are two ways to read the same result; neither of them decides anything the library does not."

## ADR-151: A verdict is a value, a limit and a sentence; the limit is inclusive; the worst join is judged; a line that cannot be judged says so

**Context.** The checklist of TEST_STRATEGY.md section 8 has "a measured value, a limit and a GO/NO-GO per line", and the strategy insists that a limit the default player cannot meet is a finding and not a number to relax. A result file that only said pass or fail could not be read next to the limit it was judged by, could not be compared with a candidate, and would turn "could not tell" into either of the two.

**Decision.** Every evaluator returns `Evaluation`s ([`verdict.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/verdict.ts)): the id, the catalog row, a label, the measured `value` (null when nothing could be measured), its unit, the `limit` and its direction (`at-most`, or `at-least` for the shortest median manifest gap and the length of a soak), the `limitKey` of `LIVE_LIMITS` it was judged against, the verdict, and the **sentence a sheet prints** (the number, the limit and what it means). Limits are imported from `LIVE_LIMITS` and never copied; every evaluator takes the limits as a parameter that defaults to them, so the tests can use others, and a result file carries the snapshot of the limits it was judged by (a later revision of the policy does not rewrite an old file). The limit is **inclusive**: a value exactly at the limit is within it (the tests check a value at the limit and one step beyond it for every limit). There are four verdicts: `GO`, `NO-GO`, `INCONCLUSIVE` and `N/A` (the JS heap outside Chromium). A check's verdict is NO-GO if any criterion line is, otherwise INCONCLUSIVE if any is, otherwise GO; a defect found is a defect even when another line could not be judged, and a short soak that finds a critical error is a NO-GO. **Advisory** lines (the p95 of the joins, the latency and manifest cadence over a soak, the restoration relative to the session's own level, the errors that no injected fault explains) are evidence with a verdict of their own that decides nothing, marked "(advisory)" on the sheet, whose sentence is printed whenever it is not a GO. A line may also carry a **note**, what a reader should know about it even when it is a GO (two estimators of the drift that disagree, a relative reading that disagrees with the absolute criterion): the sheet prints the notes of every line, whatever its verdict. A time that the player never reached (the latency never came back) is **censored**: the value is the length of the observation, flagged as a lower bound, and the sentence says "at least". Two points where the implementation follows `criteria.ts` and not the brief's wording: the start limit judges the **worst join** (`startMaxMs`: "the maximum is compared", because a member only has their own), with the p95 printed next to it as an advisory line; and the drift is the fitted slope's change over the soak (ADR-156), not the difference of two medians.

**Alternatives considered.** Pass or fail only (the three situations above are indistinguishable). Counting INCONCLUSIVE as a pass when nothing failed, or as a fail (the first lets a gate go green because it measured nothing, the second makes a 60-second harness check look like a defect of the build). Judging the p95 of five joins (type 7 with five values is a point four-fifths of the way from the fourth to the fifth: an interpolated figure that no member ever had, where the policy's own words compare the maximum). An exclusive limit (the policy words say "within"; a number written as 14,000 ms is a value the player may take).

**Consequences.** Exit codes follow: `npm run readiness` and `npm run soak` exit 0 for GO, 1 for NO-GO, 2 for INCONCLUSIVE or a run that could not be made (a browser that did not start, a documented gap, a usage error). The exit code of a readiness run is the verdict of configuration A in every report: a candidate that is GO next to an A that is NO-GO exits 1. A sheet has a few lines more than the table of the strategy (the advisory ones), each marked.

**Defending it.** "A gate that can only say green or red turns a missing measurement into one of them. I gave the third answer a name and made it fail the sheet, and I made the sentence part of the result, so a person reads the same words the file holds."

## ADR-152: PB-RDY-001 and PB-RDY-002: joins that are fresh and staggered, and a latency window read on the media clock

**Context.** PB-RDY-001 asks for "fresh sessions joined one after another at different positions inside a segment interval" and judges the worst start time; PB-RDY-002 judges the p95 and the maximum of the latency over "the measurement window after the first frame". What the live specs measured earlier: the latency at the first frame was 6,062 to 7,172 ms over 47 joins, the maximum over 20 s windows 6,201 to 6,960 ms ([ADR-100](e2e-live.md)); the 8 s and 10 s limits are loose against those, and part 2 revises them.

**Decision.** *Joins* ([`runStartCheck`](../../src/lab/readiness/checks.ts)). N joins (`LIVE_LIMITS.startSamples`, 5), each in a **fresh session and a fresh browser context**. The place inside the segment interval is set without a timer: a live session is created with the stream already on air for one DVR window (30 s) **plus the join's share of a segment** (`startOffsetSec` 30, 30.4, 30.8, 31.2, 31.6 for five joins), so the origin's publication clock is at a different phase when the page asks for its first manifest. The phase actually reached is read back from the request log (the first manifest request against the availability start time) and is in the result, so "different positions" is a fact in the file; it also contains the page's own boot, which moves every join by the same few hundred milliseconds. A join counts when the first frame is presented (`startupMs`, `load-requested` to `firstframe`, METRICS.md) with no critical error; a join with no first frame within 15 s is a failed join and a NO-GO of its own (the join timeout is the e2e wait, against a startup of 0.06 to 1.6 s on localhost in the e2e runs: the join latency of 6,062 to 7,172 ms over 47 joins in [ADR-100](e2e-live.md) is the 6 s delay plus the startup, and Linux WebKit in Docker was the slowest; the first plumbing run in Chromium, on a loaded workstation and so *not evidence*, saw 34 to 150 ms). **A warm-up join is made first and left out** (`warmUpJoin`, with the configuration under test, so that a candidate's idle path is warmed as well; it is in the result as `samples.warmup`): the first page of a browser process pays for the browser, not for the player, and the lab's convention since [ADR-120](perf.md) is to record that run and discard it. The first plumbing run showed why it is needed and not only polite: the defaults' first join took 188 ms and the other four 32 to 48 ms, so the "worst of 5" of the configuration that runs first was the browser's cold start, and the comparison table put it next to the candidate's 38 ms as if it were the defaults' cost. *Latency* ([`runLatencyCheck`](../../src/lab/readiness/checks.ts)). One read per second of media time for 60 s (`DEFAULT_WINDOW_SEC`: two DVR windows; a read is `waitForMediaTime`, then `metrics()`, as in PB-LIVE-002), judged by the p95 (Hyndman and Fan type 7, the lab's percentile, [ADR-121](perf.md)) and by the largest of the reads and of the **player's own maximum** since the first frame, which `timeupdate` keeps and which sees a spike between two reads. At least 20 reads are needed (below that the p95 and the maximum are the same claim twice). A window in which the media clock stops ends the check at once with a NO-GO that says when; the reads taken until then, and one at the moment it stopped, are in the result.

**Alternatives considered.** Waiting until the origin's clock reaches a phase before each join (a timer, in code that the spec calls; the offset of the session is exact and free). One page navigated five times (the second join would be a warm JIT and a warm connection pool, which no member's first join is). A latency window in wall time (a stall would shorten the media time and could hide itself). The nearest-rank percentile of the e2e helper (the sheet's percentile is the lab's, and a reader checks it against the spreadsheet function).

**Consequences.** The joins take about 6 x (boot + startup) (the warm-up and the five) and the window 60 s plus boot, so PB-RDY-001 and PB-RDY-002 cost about a minute and a half per engine. The phase is a property of the origin's clock, not of the player; the check cannot say how the player reacts to a segment published *during* its start, only that it starts well at five different phases. HLS is supported by the same procedures (`--protocol hls`); the HLS join downloads three extra video segments for the closed-caption probe ([ADR-099](e2e-live.md)), which the start time includes.

**Defending it.** "Positioning a join with the session's own parameter is exact and needs no clock, and I wrote the phase it really got into the file instead of trusting the arithmetic. The window is read on the media clock because a window that a stall can shorten is a window that can hide a stall."

## ADR-153: PB-RDY-003: recovery is measured from the injected fault, restoration is a tail that holds, and the defaults are a NO-GO

**Context.** The criteria ask that the player resume within `stallRecoveryMaxMs` (the outage plus two segments, 14 s) and that latency be back within the maximum (10 s) within `latencyRestoreMaxMs` (60 s) of the recovery. [ADR-103](e2e-rec.md) measured what Shaka 5.3.3's defaults (configuration A, `streaming.liveSync` off) do after a 10 s origin outage in all four engines: one stall of 3.6 to 4.4 s at the end of the buffer, no error, and then **latency that never comes back**: 10.05 to 10.64 s at the resume and 10.09 to 10.64 s two seconds later (a scratch run of 40 s in Chromium: 10.07 s at the resume, then 10.12 s at +5, +10, +20, +30 and +40 s, playback rate 1, no `live-edge-seek`). The limit is therefore missed in every run by 0.05 to 0.64 s, and that margin is the first thing to understand about the check: the arithmetic of the policy makes the unrestored latency of a 10 s outage **exactly** the maximum (the player lives on 6 s of buffer, so it stalls for 10 - 6 = 4 s, and with nothing to catch up its latency ends at the 6 s delay plus those 4 s, which is the 10 s that `latencyMaxMs` is: target plus two segments). A defect that the limit separates from health by half a second is a fragile discriminator, and the right length of the injected outage is an open question that part 2 answers with data (ADR-159). **The first plumbing runs say that this arithmetic is the least the defaults miss the limit by, not the amount** (a loaded workstation, so *plumbing, not evidence*): the check's stall, `buffering-start` to `playing`, was 4.95 to 4.99 s in 13 outages of three runs that all began at the same phase of the origin's clock, with 6.06 s of latency before, and the latency left was 11.02 s, a second above the maximum and not at it; the soak's one outage left 10.52 s (a `buffering` episode of 4.4 s); PB-REC-008 saw 10.05 to 10.64 s. A stall is at least the part of the outage that the buffer does not cover (10 s less the 6 s the player lives on: 4 s), and the stall of the check was 4.85 to 4.99 s: about a second more, which was not looked into (a hypothesis: the time the player takes to see the origin back, a manifest refresh away). **The phase did not move it**: in a second plumbing run the three outages of the check, staggered inside the segment interval (below), stalled 4.85 s (the first page of a cold browser), 4.98 s and 4.98 s, so at those three positions on the origin's clock the stall was the same to within 2 ms, and the 3.6 to 4.4 s of PB-REC-008 is not explained by where the player started. How much of a second the defaults miss the limit by, and what moves it, is a question for part 2.

**Decision.** *The procedure* ([`runStallCheck`](../../src/lab/readiness/checks.ts)). In a fresh session the player plays 4 s of media past its first frame, the check reads the latency three times, one read per media second and the last where the 4 s end, and takes their median as the session's own steady level (the level before), reads the origin's edge, and installs `originStall(edge + 3, LIVE_LIMITS.injectedStallMs)` as the recovery spec does ([ADR-103](e2e-rec.md)): the origin stops publishing four to six seconds from now and stays silent for 10 s. The runs are **staggered inside the segment interval** like the joins of ADR-152 (the session of run *i* of *n* is created `(i - 1) / n` of a segment later on the origin's clock: 30, 30.67 and 31.33 s on air for three), because the position of the player on the origin's publication clock could change when it sees the origin back and three runs at one position would be one measurement three times; one plumbing run did not find that it does (ADR-153, Context), and the stagger stays because it costs nothing and the other engines were not looked at. It waits for `buffering-start` after the cursor it took before installing and reads the origin's own state at that moment (`live.stalled` must be true: the proof that the fault was in force, [ADR-101](e2e-rec.md)); a run in which the player never ran dry, or ran dry while the origin was not stalled, exercised no recovery and is **INCONCLUSIVE**, never a pass. It waits for `playing`; the recovery is `buffering-start` to that `playing` on the page's own clock, and a player that does not resume within the limit plus the e2e slack is a NO-GO (a hung player). From the recovery on it reads latency once per second of media time, stamped with the harness's wall clock since the recovery, until latency has been within the maximum **and stayed there for 5 s** and (the relative reading below) within a segment of the session's own level for the same hold, or until the limit and the hold are over (65 s). The check makes three runs (`DEFAULT_STALL_RUNS`; "the worst of the repeated runs is judged") and judges the worst recovery and the worst restoration. *Restoration* is the start of the longest tail of the observation in which every read is within `latencyMaxMs`, provided the tail lasts the hold ([`restoreFrom`](../../src/lab/readiness/evaluators.ts)): a single read under the maximum is a dip and not a player that came back, and an observation that ends too soon after crossing says nothing. A run that never gets there is censored ("at least 65.0 s") and NO-GO when it was watched for at least the limit, INCONCLUSIVE when it was not. The sentence of a NO-GO says where the player stood and how much further behind than before the outage it is. *The relative reading.* The same tail is also asked relative to the session's own level: restored when every read is within one segment (`RELATIVE_TOLERANCE_MS`, 2 s) of the steady level the session held before the outage, held for the same 5 s ([`restoreWithin`](../../src/lab/readiness/evaluators.ts)). It is an **advisory line** (`stall.restore-relative`, "Latency at its own level") next to the criterion and never decides it: the verdict of PB-RDY-003 is the absolute limit, exactly as the strategy words it. Why a second reading at all: the arithmetic above makes the unrestored latency of a 10 s outage **exactly** the maximum, so the absolute limit separates the defaults from health by 0.05 to 0.64 s (the defaults resume at 10.05 to 10.64 s behind the edge, a healthy session sits at 6.2 to 7.2 s, [ADR-100](e2e-live.md) and [ADR-103](e2e-rec.md)); a reading relative to the session's own level separates them by what the outage leaves behind (3.9 to 4.4 s further behind than the session was) less the tolerance, and the tolerance of one segment is half of it. It also treats a session that sat high (7.2 s) and one that sat low (6.1 s) alike, which the absolute limit does not. The costs of the second reading: the session's own level is the median of three reads taken in four seconds, so it is an estimate; and the observation lasts until both readings are restored, so the candidate is watched for about twenty seconds longer. **Which of the two discriminates better is not decided here, and no limit changes**: Phase 7 part 2 chooses from the measured values, how far apart the two populations are in each reading (the defaults and the candidate, every engine, the runs of one engine against each other), and until then the sheet prints both and where they disagree about a run it says so in a note on the relative line. *The candidate.* The checks take a configuration patch, and the result carries what `qa.info().configured` says the page applied. **The patch has to be in force before Shaka loads, and a page that is given a manifest loads at once.** Shaka 5.3.3 registers the `timeupdate` listener that runs its live-sync rate control (`Player.onTimeUpdate_`) inside `load()`, and only when `streaming.liveSync.enabled` is on at that moment (`lib/player.js`: the block `if ((isDynamic && ((this.config_.streaming.liveSync && this.config_.streaming.liveSync.enabled) || ...` that calls `this.loadEventManager_.listen(mediaElement, 'timeupdate', onTimeUpdate)`, once on the media-source path and once on the `src=` path); `player.configure()` does not add the listener afterwards. So the checks open a candidate's page **idle** (no `manifest` parameter: the page builds Shaka and waits, as `PlayerPageParams.manifest` says), call `qa.configure()`, and only then `qa.load(manifest)` and `qa.play()`, the two started inside the page in one call (`bootPlayer` in [`checks.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/checks.ts)); the defaults are opened with the manifest, the way a viewer's page opens. **The first version of the checks did it the other way round**, opened the page with the manifest and configured afterwards, which sets the key and changes nothing. The first plumbing run on real browsers showed it, and no unit test could have: the candidate's outages were no better restored than the defaults' (the first plumbing run, a workstation at load average 4.9, so *plumbing, not evidence*: the defaults stalled 4.96 to 4.99 s with 6.06 s of latency before and still 11.02 s after 65 s; the candidate's first two outages stalled 4.98 s and ended at 11.02 s after 65 s too), and reading Shaka's source found why. The fake of the unit tests had caught up whenever a patch had been applied at any time, so it passed the wrong order; it now models what Shaka does (the rate control exists only if the option was on when the load started, a page opened with the manifest loads before anything can be configured, and a page that nothing told to load and play presents no frame), and mutating `bootPlayer` back to the old order turns nine tests in six files red. **What this does not settle:** the candidate's join is measured from `qa.load()` as the defaults' is (`load-requested` is the hook's own mark of the call, whichever way it was made) and the harness's own round trip is not inside it, but the defaults' load starts from the page's own start-up code and the candidate's from a call through the debugging protocol. Part 2 should measure that path before it compares join times of A and a candidate at the level of milliseconds, and there is a control that costs a minute: `npm run readiness -- --checks 1 --config-json '{"streaming":{"liveSync":{"enabled":false}}}'` evaluates A and then A plus its own default value through the idle path, which behaves as A. What Shaka 5.3.3 offers is read from the installed source and not guessed: `streaming.liveSync` has `enabled`, `targetLatency` (0.5 s), `targetLatencyTolerance` (0.5 s), `maxPlaybackRate` (1.1), `minPlaybackRate` (0.95), `panicMode`, `panicThreshold` and `dynamicTargetLatency` (`externs/shaka/player.js`, defaults in `lib/util/player_configuration.js`). Its latency is `seekRange().end - currentTime` (`Player.onTimeUpdate_`), which is the hook's latency minus the 6 s presentation delay: after the outage the playhead is about 4.05 to 4.64 s behind Shaka's target, which is above `targetLatency + tolerance` = 1 s, so with live sync on the player should play at 1.1 until it is back, gaining 0.1 s per second: **by reading the source**, the hook's latency should come back at 0.1 s per second: from the 10.05 to 10.64 s that the e2e runs left, under the 10 s maximum within about 0.5 to 6.4 s of the recovery, and to about 7 s after about 30 to 36 s. **What the plumbing run of Phase B saw** (a loaded workstation: *plumbing, not evidence*, a hypothesis until part 2): the stall of the check left 11.02 s, and with live sync on the latency was under the maximum 10.99 to 11.01 s after the recovery and within 2 s of its own level 30.08 to 30.10 s after it, in all three outages, which is a little over what 0.1 s per second gives from 11.02 s (10.2 s and 29.6 s); the 0.8 s more is not explained here (the reads are one per second of media time, and how soon after the recovery Shaka raises the rate was not looked at), and the defaults stayed at 11.02 s for the 65 s they were watched. So the mechanism read in the source is the one at work; whether it holds in the other engines, at other phases and on a quiet machine is part 2's. The one candidate is therefore **A plus `streaming.liveSync.enabled: true`** ([`candidates.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/candidates.ts), `live-sync`: its patch is type-checked against Shaka's own declaration of its configuration, and it carries a falsifiable hypothesis, like the knobs of configuration B), with Shaka's own values for the rest. `npm run readiness -- --candidate live-sync` evaluates A first and then the candidate and prints them side by side; `PB_READINESS_CANDIDATE=live-sync` makes the spec judge the candidate. Whether it really recovers is a part 2 measurement; the unit tests only show that a world whose rate control exists only if the option was on when the player loaded judges a candidate applied before the load GO, and the same patch applied after it as the defaults are judged.

**Alternatives considered.** Reading the recovery from the request log (the stall fails no request, the log cannot name it, [ADR-084](server.md)). Judging the latency "at the resume" (it is the same 10.1 s in A and in a fixed player for the first seconds; the check is about the time to get back). One run (a restoration that is borderline in one run in three is the thing the repetition is for). Bending the limit or the assertion so that A passes: a limit the default cannot meet is a finding (`criteria.ts` says so), and the spec is red for A for that reason. The seek-to-live-edge action of the hook as a "fix" (a candidate is a player configuration; the check must not do the player's job).

**Consequences.** PB-RDY-003 is **NO-GO for configuration A** by what the earlier phase measured, and the checklist says so with the numbers; the release spec for it is red for A until the configuration changes (an A/B decision, checklist item 6) or the owner accepts the finding in writing. It takes about 86 s a run for A (the lead of about 12 s, the outage, and the whole 65 s of observation), so 4.3 minutes for three runs; the candidate, which stops observing once latency is back within a segment of where it was and has stayed there for the hold, takes about 57 s a run (observation of 35.5 s: 30.1 s to come back within 2 s of its own level and 5 s of hold), 2.8 minutes for three (measured in the plumbing run of Phase B, on a loaded workstation: the durations of part 2 replace them).

**Defending it.** "The check is built to be able to say no, and it says no to the defaults, with a reason that a person can follow in two lines of arithmetic and a sentence about a configuration key that I read in the source. It is also built so that the fix is a configuration I can put next to it and measure, and it does not pass the fix on my say-so."

## ADR-154: PB-RDY-004: the cadence is what the origin saw, gap by gap, outside the faults

**Context.** The criterion is the median and the longest gap between consecutive manifest (or media playlist) requests in the request log, outside injected faults: the median must not be shorter than half the advertised period (polling faster than advertised multiplies the load at the scale of an event) and the longest not longer than two periods (the player has stopped tracking the window). The live specs recorded 1,931 to 2,403 ms between refreshes over fourteen runs, medians 1,999 to 2,007 ms ([ADR-099](e2e-live.md)).

**Decision.** The check watches 20 refreshes after the load (`DEFAULT_REFRESHES`, 40 s of the origin's clock) by waiting on the server's log (`waitForLog`, the lab's one polling primitive) for 21 requests of the refresh series, and reads the gaps from the **arrival times** of the requests at the origin, not from anything the player says about itself. For DASH the series is every manifest request; for HLS it is the media playlists of the audio rendition, which has one (the video playlist is another file after every rung switch, and a switch is not a refresh: [ADR-099](e2e-live.md)); the master playlist, requested once, is not part of it. A request counts when it **arrives**, whatever its answer. Gaps that overlap an injected fault window are left out (the function takes the windows; PB-RDY-004's own session has none, and the soak uses it for its advisory cadence line). At least ten gaps are needed to judge. A player that has not asked 21 times in the limit's time (`refreshes + 5` periods and 30 s) is a NO-GO ("it stopped tracking the live window") with the gaps seen so far.

**Alternatives considered.** The player's own refresh events (the hook has none: the origin's log is the second channel precisely so that the player is not graded by itself, [ADR-098](e2e-live.md)). Completion times instead of arrival times (a slow answer would stretch the gap before it and shorten the one after). Counting every manifest-kind request including the master playlist of HLS (it would put one request, without a successor, in the middle of the series).

**Consequences.** The check says nothing about the *size* of a manifest or about the load an origin carries; it counts requests. Its resolution is the log's: arrival times are in milliseconds, and the service stamps them when the request reaches it.

**Defending it.** "The cadence is a fact of the origin's log. I read it there, so that a player that stops asking cannot say it did not."

## ADR-155: The soak harness: a sampler that lives in the page, a driver that can be replaced, and a soak that waits for a stalled player before it ends

**Context.** The soak has to survive its own length ("a soak that leaks in the harness proves nothing"), has to keep watching while the player is stalled (a hang is what it exists to find), and has to be testable without thirty minutes of real time. A sampler driven by a timer of the harness is delayed by whatever the harness is doing (a forced collection through DevTools takes hundreds of milliseconds) and is stamped with the harness's clock, and a wait on the media clock does not return while the clock is stopped.

**Decision.** The sampler is a **string of JavaScript that the harness evaluates in the page** ([`sampler.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/sampler.ts)): a `setInterval` of the page (every 5 s by default) reads the hook's `metrics()` and keeps the read, stamped with the page's own clock (`performance.now()`, the clock of the event timeline), in a list that the harness **drains** every 30 s (the list is emptied by the drain, bounded to 2,000 reads with the loss counted, so the page holds a few dozen reads however long the soak). Times are converted to **epoch milliseconds** with the page's `performance.timeOrigin`, so that the player's reads, the origin's log (ISO times) and the injected faults (the publication instant of a segment) are on one axis. It is a string and not a function because the harness is compiled by `tsx`, which keeps function names with a helper that does not exist in the page, and because a string can be run in a Node `vm` with a fake `window`: the unit test ticks the interval by hand ([`sampler.test.ts`](../../tests/unit/readiness/sampler.test.ts)). The loop ([`runSoak`](../../src/lab/readiness/soak.ts)) is written against `SoakDriver` (start, sample, drain, snapshot, read the heap, read the harness's memory, report a crashed page, stop): the command implements it over Playwright, the unit tests over a **virtual clock** that releases a recorded series as time passes, so a thirty-minute soak, a hung player and a page that disappears run through the real loop in milliseconds. The outages are **rules of the session installed before the page exists** (`originStall(edge + n, 10 s)` at the segment that is published `minutes` after creation; `staleManifest(10 s, afterMs)`, whose clock starts at the first media request), so no timer injects them and their windows are known exactly on the epoch axis. The session lives for the soak plus 15 minutes (the default of 15 minutes would expire under a 30-minute soak and end it in 404s that look like a defect). The loop reads the memory at the first frame, every 30 s, once at the end of the warm-up and once at the end, and drains the sampler and the origin's log after every sleep. **When the soak's time is up and the player is stalled, it does not end**: it gives the player the stall-recovery limit to get going, so that "a stall that never ends" is a fact and not an accident of when the clock ran out. A page that crashes or is closed ends the soak at once, as a critical error (`PAGE_GONE`), so it is a NO-GO and not a missing result.

**Alternatives considered.** A timer of the harness reading `metrics()` every 5 s (the delays above, and a stalled page would make the reads wait). The sampler as an exported function evaluated by Playwright (the transformer problem, and nothing to run it in without a browser). Reading the whole timeline at the end only (it would show a stall but not the series of latencies that drift needs). Installing the outage from the harness at the chosen minute (a timer to be on time, and a window to be measured instead of computed).

**Consequences.** The harness's own memory is read at every memory reading (its heap after a collection it forces at run time with `v8.setFlagsFromString('--expose-gc')`, its resident size, and the resident size of the service it started), in the result file, so that a leak in the harness shows. **The five-minute dry run of the brief that checks it was made in Phase B** (`npm run soak -- --minutes ci`, Chromium 156.0.8078.4 on the Mac, a workstation shared with other agents at a load average of 2.5 to 6.0 on 12 CPUs, so *plumbing, not evidence*; 5.3 minutes from the start of the command to its end, of which the soak is 5.0 from the first frame). It was the first time that the sampler string, the DevTools session, the digest of the origin's log and the readings of the harness's memory ran in a real page, and they did what the fake said they would: the sampler installed and drained 61 reads (one per 5 s) under the page's own content security policy, the heap was read 11 times (at the first frame and every 30 s), the digest counted 459 requests (151 manifests, 2 init segments, 306 media segments, none refused), the outage at minute 2.5 was a `buffering` episode of 4.4 s and the verdict was GO. **What memory the run measured, and what it did not:** the page's JS heap (`Performance.getMetrics` `JSHeapUsedSize` after a forced `HeapProfiler.collectGarbage`: 3.10 MB at the first frame, 3.7 to 3.9 MB from the minute on, 3.83 MB at the end, +0.1 MB over the 9 readings after the warm-up, 232 DOM nodes and 64 listeners at every reading), the harness's own heap after a forced collection (49.8 MB, then 50.4, 50.8, ... 51.4 MB: +1.6 MB in five minutes, 0.6 of it in the first 30 s), and the resident sizes of the harness (191 MB at the first reading, about 100 MB from minute 1.5, 67 MB at the end) and of the service (90 MB, then 75, 65 ... 57 MB, 29 MB at the end), which **fall** over the run; the resident size is not the memory that the process holds, and on macOS it falls when the system compresses an idle process, which is a plausible reading of these numbers and not something that was checked. The JS heap is not the player's memory: Shaka's media buffers live in the browser's media pipeline, outside it, and **no claim about the memory of the player beyond the JS heap is made**; the harness's growth of 1.6 MB in five minutes is the figure to compare a 30-minute soak against, not a limit. The sampler string and the loop around it had also run, over the fake page of ADR-150 on a virtual clock, for a CI soak, a soak whose page crashes at minute two, a soak outside Chromium and a soak with a stale manifest; the string is evaluated there by the same rules as in the page (a scope with the page's globals only, the result by structured clone), so a reference to something of the harness's would fail. **What no real run has exercised yet:** the stale manifest of a soak (the default of the CI soak injects none), a soak outside Chromium, HLS, and a candidate soak. The in-page sampler's `metrics()` call does the work of the hook for every read (the timeline is small: a few dozen events in half an hour), so it is part of the load the page carries, one read per 5 s. A page that was reloaded or whose sampler was replaced fails the drain with a clear message (the soak is then a NO-GO with the reason).

**Defending it.** "I put the sampler where the thing it samples lives, and the loop where a test can drive it. A soak that can only be tested by running it is a soak whose own bugs I find at minute twenty-nine."

## ADR-156: The soak's evaluators: drift is a robust slope with a level and a cross-check, an outage is a cut and not a slope, and a stall has two witnesses

**Context.** The criteria of PB-RDY-005 were six lines when this record was written (unrecovered stalls, critical errors, rebuffer ratio, latency drift, JS heap growth and dropped frames) and are seven since ADR-156a added the critical errors that no injected fault explains. The strategy defined the drift as the median of the last quarter minus the median of the first quarter after the warm-up; the brief asked for the slope of a least-squares line **with the intercept**, "so that a high but flat latency is not called drift". The first version of this record kept that, and found its weakness in review: a least-squares slope gives a read at the end of the series a leverage of `6(n-1)/(n(n+1))`, 0.12 for the 49 reads of a CI soak after its warm-up, so a single read 8.5 s too high at the very end (14.7 s on a series at 6.2 s) is enough to read 1 s of drift in a player that never drifted. The owner decided, for Phase A.2, that the verdict is the **Theil-Sen** estimator, with least squares kept beside it as a cross-check and the sheet saying when the two disagree. The soak also injects an outage whose aftermath (the defaults stay 4 s further behind for good) is a step in the series, and a stale manifest in a release soak.

**Decision.** *Drift* ([`evaluateLatencyDrift`](../../src/lab/readiness/soak-evaluators.ts), [`regression.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/regression.ts)). The **Theil-Sen** line through the latency reads after the warm-up ([`pooledTheilSen`](../../src/lab/readiness/regression.ts)): its slope is the median of the slopes between every two reads of the same stretch, its **level** the median of `y - slope * x` (the median-of-intercepts line, reported at the start of the window), and the verdict is the **change the slope makes over the length of the soak after the warm-up**, in the unit of the limit (`maxLatencyDriftMs`, 1 s). A flat series at any level has a slope of zero, so a player that sits 10 s behind steadily is not drifting (that is PB-RDY-003's finding); a series that climbs is, whatever it started at. Both signs count (a latency that falls steadily is a media clock that runs fast and ends at the edge). **Ordinary least squares** (computed on values centred on their means: the x is an epoch time of 1.7e12) is the cross-check: its change over the same stretches is in the evidence and in the sentence, and a **note** is printed on the sheet, whatever the verdict, when the two differ by more than the limit itself (the margin a verdict has: a gap that large can be the whole difference between GO and NO-GO) or would not give the same verdict. The slope is also reported in ms per hour, and the difference of the medians of the first and last quarter is a second cross-check (`quartileShiftMs`, for a series without a cut). The policy text of `criteria.ts` and the strategy say this; the tests pin that they do ([`policy-text.test.ts`](../../tests/unit/readiness/policy-text.test.ts)). *Outages.* The fit **leaves out the settling window of every injected fault**: from the outage's start until the stall-recovery limit after it ends plus the time the policy gives the player to get back to the edge (`latencyRestoreMaxMs`), so that the ramp of a player that catches up is not read as a slope. What is left is cut into stretches, and **one slope is fitted to all of them with a level of each of its own**: the pairs of reads are only taken inside a stretch (for least squares, the within-stretch estimator of a fixed-effects regression, `pooledSlope`), so the step an outage leaves is never a slope (one line through both sides of it reads it as one: least squares wherever it falls, a Theil-Sen over all pairs when it falls in the middle of the reads, where half of the pairs straddle it; the unit tests have both), and all the fault-free time contributes to the one slope. The drift needs at least 12 reads over at least two fault-free minutes, otherwise it is INCONCLUSIVE (two minutes because a slope fitted on much less says nothing about four minutes: with 25 reads, 5 s apart, over two minutes, the standard error of the change that the slope makes over the 4 minutes of a CI soak is about 0.13 s for 100 ms of scatter in the reads and about 0.4 s for 300 ms, which is a good part of the limit at the second figure; the 49 reads of a whole CI soak bring it to 0.05 s and 0.15 s; these are least squares' figures, and under normal scatter the Theil-Sen slope's are about 2 % larger, its asymptotic relative efficiency being 3/π; part 2 checks it against the scatter that is measured). A stretch of more than 1,200 reads (719,400 pairs held in memory at once) is thinned to evenly spaced reads and the result says so; no soak of the lengths of the policy comes near it. *Stalls.* An unrecovered stall is found by **two witnesses**: the hook's `buffering` episodes that are longer than the recovery limit or still open (the hook leaves out a stall that begins inside a seek the player made itself, METRICS.md), and a **media clock that stood still** for longer than the limit while the player was not paused (a hung player stops the clock whatever the hook thinks); overlapping stretches count once. *The rebuffer ratio* is the stall time of the episodes that begin after the warm-up and outside an injected fault (extended by the recovery limit), over the time observed outside them (METRICS.md's ratio, since a soak never pauses or seeks). *Errors:* critical errors (`maxCriticalErrors`, whole soak) are a criterion, and so are the critical errors that no injected fault explains (`maxUnexplainedCriticalErrors`, [ADR-156a](#adr-156a-unexplained-critical-errors-are-a-criterion-and-unexplained-recoverable-errors-are-reported)); the recoverable errors that no injected fault explains are an advisory line with counts and first examples. *Dropped frames:* dropped over decoded at the end of the soak. *Length:* a soak shorter than the CI length is INCONCLUSIVE, and the kind of soak (`short`, `ci`, `release`) is in the result and on the sheet; the checklist counts only a `release` soak for its item 4.

**Alternatives considered.** The strategy's difference of medians as the verdict (two numbers of the series decide, and an outage's step makes it a verdict about the stall). One line through the whole series (the step above). Excluding only the outage's own seconds (the ramp after it would be a drift). **Least squares as the verdict**, which the brief asked for, and which this record had until the review found its leverage: a read that is too high by `x` at the very end of `n` reads moves the fitted change by `6(n-1)/(n(n+1))` of `x`, which is 0.12 for the 49 reads of a CI soak after its warm-up and 0.017 for the 349 of a release soak, so a single read 8.5 s too high at the very end of a CI soak is enough to read 1 s of drift, and in the release soak it takes one 58 s too high (in the middle of the series the leverage is nearly nothing, which is why the first unit test of a wild read, a spike at 200 s, passed on least squares). A healthy series has no such read (the live specs saw 6.2 to 7.0 s), but a stall that is going on when the soak ends is exactly where one would be, and a verdict must not hang on one read. Least squares stays as the cross-check, where its sensitivity is a feature: it is what shows a climb that the median cannot. Other robust fits (the repeated median, which survives half of the reads being wild but is harder to explain and to test; Huber or RANSAC fits, which have a tuning constant or a random seed that a result file would have to carry). A rate limit in ms per hour (the policy's unit is a change over the soak; whether the limit should be a rate that is the same for a 5-minute and a 30-minute soak is a decision for part 2).

**Consequences.** The limit is the same 1 s for a 5-minute and for a 30-minute soak, so the *rate* it permits is six times larger in the short one: the CI soak fails a drift above about 15 s per hour (1 s over its 4 minutes after the warm-up), the release soak above about 2 s per hour (1 s over 29 minutes), and ADR-158 says what that leaves unseen. The sheet shows the slope and the least-squares reading, so a reader can see both. **What the choice costs.** The median of the pairwise slopes does not see a ramp that covers less than about 29 % of the reads (its breakdown point: more than half of the pairs lie in the flat part), so a series that is flat for three quarters of the soak and then climbs 8 s in the last quarter reads as no drift, while least squares reads 5.8 s. The unit test `flags a series that is flat and then climbs in the last part` pins exactly that: the verdict stays the robust one and the sheet prints the note, which is then the only place the climb shows. Whether a disagreement should itself make the line INCONCLUSIVE is a question for part 2, which will know from real series how often the two differ.

**Decided (Phase B, by the owner): the errors that no injected fault explains.** TEST_STRATEGY.md section 8 said "anything outside an injected fault window fails the soak" while `LIVE_READINESS` had no row for it. The owner decided that a part of it is a criterion and the rest is reported: see [ADR-156a](#adr-156a-unexplained-critical-errors-are-a-criterion-and-unexplained-recoverable-errors-are-reported). The `Errors` line of this record's decision, which printed everything outside the faults as information, is superseded by it.

**Defending it.** "A drift is a trend and not a level, and an outage is a cut and not a trend. I fitted what the words say with a line that one wild read cannot move, I put the classical fit next to it so that a reader sees when they differ, and when the series has a step in it I say where it is and who owns it."

## ADR-156a: Unexplained critical errors are a criterion, and unexplained recoverable errors are reported

(An amendment of ADR-156, written in Phase B. The range ADR-150 to ADR-159 of this phase is full and ADR-160 and up belong to the triage phase, so it carries a letter and not a number; renumber it at the merge if the project prefers.)

**Context.** The strategy's list of what the soak monitors said that "anything outside an injected fault window fails the soak"; the criteria had one row about errors (`maxCriticalErrors`, zero: any critical error, whatever explains it). Phase A and A.2 printed the errors that no injected fault explains as information, said that a criterion was warranted, and left the decision to the owner. A live event is run by people who cannot reproduce what happens in it. An error with a cause they put there (an origin that was stopped on purpose) is part of the test; an error with no cause anyone knows is either a defect or an effect of the environment that nobody has understood, and "go" cannot be signed on a soak that contains something nobody can attribute.

**Decision.** (1) **What is unexplained.** A player or page error event, that is an entry of `qa.metrics().errors` (Shaka's errors, and the page's own: an uncaught exception, an unhandled rejection, a refused `play()`, an unusable parameter), whose time is **not inside the window of a fault that the scenario injected**: from the fault's start until the stall-recovery limit (14 s) after it ends. The grace is the time the policy gives the player to resume after the origin returns, so an error of the retry that follows the outage is the outage's; it is the window that already excludes those stalls from the rebuffer ratio. The lab knows the windows exactly, because the faults are rules of the session that it placed itself ([`unexplainedErrors`](../../src/lab/readiness/soak-evaluators.ts)). (2) **Unexplained critical errors (severity 2) are a criterion**: `LIVE_READINESS.maxUnexplainedCriticalErrors`, **zero**, on the sheet as "Unexplained critical errors", and one of them blocks GO. (3) **Unexplained recoverable errors** (severity 1, and the page's own errors, which are severity 1) are **reported, with their counts by name and their first three examples** (when in minutes of the soak, what, where it was caught, what it said), on an advisory line that decides nothing. (4) **The row of every critical error stays**: a critical error inside a fault's window is a recovery that failed and still fails the soak. The new row cannot fail alone, because an unexplained critical error is also a critical error; what it adds is the attribution (the sentence says that nothing explains it, names it, and gives the first example) and the statement of the policy. If the owner wants an *explained* critical error to be tolerated, the older row is the one to relax, and that is a decision about recovery and not about attribution.

**What the failure drives.** A no-go on the soak, so on item 4 of the checklist ("no critical error and none that no injected fault explains"), and an investigation that starts at **the first example**: the sentence prints its minute, its name and code, where it was caught and its message, and the result file keeps the first three on the epoch axis, next to the digest of the origin's request log and the series, so that the log and the player's timeline can be read around it. It is not a limit to relax and not a quarantine: a critical error that nobody can attribute is a finding until somebody has attributed it.

**Alternatives considered.** Any unexplained error blocks (what the strategy's sentence said): on a real network a retried segment is ordinary, and a soak that failed on each of them would be a flaky gate in the one place a gate must be trusted; the recoverable errors are kept visible with their counts instead, so that a trend is seen. Information only (Phase A.2): it let a critical error that nothing explains be one line among others. Replacing the row of every critical error by this one: a critical error inside a fault's window would pass, and that is a player that did not survive the injected failure. A longer grace: errors that the fault no longer plausibly causes would be explained by it. The fault's own seconds only: the retries after the origin returns are the outage's and would be flagged.

**Consequences.** The soak row has seven criteria and one more advisory line. A fault the harness injected and did not hand to the evaluators would make its errors unexplained, which is why the driver records the window of every rule it installs ([`soak-driver.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/soak-driver.ts)); and an error that the harness itself causes (the page that crashed under it, `PAGE_GONE`) is a critical error that nothing injected, so it is a no-go, which is intended. The unexplained recoverable errors are the first thing to read in a soak that passed: a few of them in a five-minute soak on localhost, where no network is to blame, would be a result of their own for part 2. Where the examples go: `detail.first` of each line.

**Defending it.** "I do not need every error to be zero, and I cannot afford one I cannot explain. The fault I injected is a cause I can name; a critical error outside it has none, so it is a no-go and the first example is where I start. The recoverable ones I count and show, because real networks make them and a trend is worth more than a veto."

## ADR-157: The JS heap is read after a forced collection, at both ends and between, and the harness's own memory is read with it

**Context.** The heap of a running page is a sawtooth between collections: a reading taken from the teeth is noise, and growth read from it is a lottery. What the page retains is what is left after a collection, so growth there is a leak. Only Chromium offers it (DevTools protocol): Firefox and WebKit are judged on the other signals, and the criterion says "N/A" there instead of inventing a number.

**Decision.** [`heap.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/heap.ts) enables the Performance domain once, and for every reading sends `HeapProfiler.collectGarbage` (which resolves when the collection is done) and only then `Performance.getMetrics`, taking `JSHeapUsedSize` in MB (2^20 bytes) with the DOM `Nodes` and `JSEventListeners` counts beside it, which say what grew when the heap did. *The order of the calls was read, then observed.* In the protocol definition of the V8 inspector (`pdl/js_protocol.pdl` of ChromeDevTools/devtools-protocol, master at b0d96c6, domain HeapProfiler) `collectGarbage` takes no parameter and is not documented as depending on `enable`, and Playwright 1.64.1's own `requestGC()` (`playwright-core/lib/coreBundle.js`) sends `HeapProfiler.collectGarbage` alone. The protocol does not say what `Performance.getMetrics` answers before `Performance.enable`, so it was **observed** ([`live/cdp-heap-probe.ts`](../../live/cdp-heap-probe.ts), Chromium 156.0.8078.4 through Playwright 1.64.1, headless, macOS arm64, on the shared workstation: a property of the API and not of the timing, so the load does not matter; the output is kept in the log of that run): **`getMetrics` before `Performance.enable` returned 0 metrics, and 36 after it, `JSHeapUsedSize`, `Nodes` and `JSEventListeners` among them** (the claim "Chromium answers an empty list before `enable`" is confirmed); **`HeapProfiler.collectGarbage` with no `HeapProfiler.enable` worked**: `JSHeapUsedSize` fell from 81 MB, after 60 MB of unreachable arrays, to 0.5 MB; **`Performance.enable` is per DevTools session** (a second session that had not enabled it got 0 metrics) **and the collection is the isolate's**: after the second session collected, the first read 0.5 MB instead of 53.9 MB; and **the order matters as the soak needs**: three rounds of "make garbage, collect, read" read 0.5, 0.5 and 0.5 MB, and of "make garbage, read, collect" 53.9, 53.9 and 53.9 MB, which is what a reading without the collection first would have put in the series. The probe turns an answer without `JSHeapUsedSize` into an error that names the metrics it did get. The fake session of the unit tests answers as these observations say (an empty list before `enable`, the garbage since the last collection included in a reading that was not preceded by one, an error for a method the protocol does not have), so a missing `Performance.enable` and a `getMetrics` before the collection each fail a test. A reading the page did not give is an error and never a zero. The growth is the **median of the first readings after the warm-up** against the **median of the last** (up to three at each end: one collection can land on a trough or a peak), at least four readings after the warm-up; the trend of the whole series (MB per hour and the level it starts from) is in the evidence, so that a steady climb is told from one step. The readings are at the first frame, every 30 s (`--heap-every-sec`), at the end of the warm-up and at the end, with the whole series in the file (decimated to 300 points with the peaks kept, [`series.ts`](../../src/lab/readiness/series.ts)). The forced collection is a perturbation (a full mark-compact of a heap of a few megabytes, tens of milliseconds, and it can flush the bytecode of cold functions), which is why it runs every 30 s and not every sample. The harness's own memory is read at the same moments: the heap of the command's process after a collection it forces itself, its resident size, and the resident size of the service it started (`ps -o rss=`).

**Alternatives considered.** The heap as the page reports it without a collection (a sawtooth). `performance.memory` (a Chromium extension that is quantised and rounded, and does not force a collection). Heap snapshots (a snapshot is seconds of work and megabytes of data per reading, and moves the thing it measures). One reading at each end (one collection can land on a peak: the median of up to three is the cheap cure).

**Consequences.** The player and the origin exchange two segments every 2 s (one video, one audio), 3,600 an hour, so a leak of 1 KB per segment is 3.6 MB an hour: 0.3 MB in the CI soak and 1.8 MB in the release soak. The 20 MB of the criterion is therefore a detector of leaks of about 11 KB per segment or more in thirty minutes, and of about 67 KB or more in five: a *gross*-leak detector in the CI soak and a medium one in the release soak, which is what the policy text already says (its basis, "a few kilobytes of bookkeeping per segment", would stay under it for hours); the real distribution of the heap after collection is a part 2 measurement, and a limit that no leak of a size that matters could cross would be too loose. DOM nodes and listeners are the first place to look when the heap grows, and are in the file. **The plumbing CI soak of Phase B** (Chromium, a loaded workstation, *not evidence*) read 3.7 MB after the warm-up and 3.8 MB at the end, +0.1 MB over nine readings (a trend of +2.5 MB an hour) with 232 nodes and 64 listeners throughout: the limit of 20 MB is about 190 times the growth seen, which says what the criterion is (a detector of a leak of a few megabytes a minute) and not that it is wrong; part 2 decides with a leak put in on purpose.

**Defending it.** "I measured what the page keeps, not what it has not yet thrown away, and I read my own harness with the same instrument so that a leak that is mine cannot be taken for the player's."

## ADR-158: What a 5-minute or a 30-minute soak can and cannot say about an event of hours

**Context.** A live event lasts hours; the CI soak watches 5 minutes and the release soak 30 (`soakMinutesCi`, `soakMinutesRelease`). TEST_STRATEGY.md section 13 already says the result is an extrapolation. This record says how large the step is and what the evaluators can resolve.

**Decision, as a statement of what the soak says.** (1) **What it can find.** A defect that shows within the length: a drift or a leak whose size over the soak is above the limit and above the scatter of the series (a drift of a few seconds an hour is a quarter of a second in five minutes: invisible; a drift of a minute an hour is 5 s: seen), a stall after minutes, a critical error at a window slide or at the loop of the 24 s source (the source loops 12 times in five minutes and 75 in thirty, with rewritten timestamps, so a discontinuity at the loop would show), a failure to recover from an outage in the middle of a session (the outage is injected at a chosen minute, so recovery *mid-soak* is part of what is judged). (2) **What it cannot say.** What happens after the 30 minutes: a leak of 1 MB an hour is 0.5 MB in the release soak and cannot be told from the sawtooth; the effects of a 3-hour clock drift between the origin's and the player's clocks (the origin here is the same machine's clock: a real encoder drifts against a real player, which the lab cannot model); memory of the *origin*; real networks. (3) **What the numbers mean.** A soak is one run on one machine: it says that this build did not fail in this session, with an uncertainty that part 2 measures by repeating it. A GO is not "the event will hold". (4) **The resolution of the drift.** The fitted change has a standard error that the report can estimate from the scatter (`scatterMs` of each stretch, a robust standard deviation, is in the evidence, and `residualSdMs` of the least-squares fit under `leastSquares`), and which is optimistic, because consecutive reads of a latency are correlated; the evidence says the scatter and the evaluator refuses to judge from less than two fault-free minutes. What the robust fit cannot resolve is a climb that covers the last quarter of the soak or less (ADR-156): that shows only as the note. (5) **The extrapolation.** The slope is reported in ms per hour, and an extrapolation to the length of an event is a multiplication that a reader can do and that the sheet does not print as a result: the sheet prints what was measured, and ADR-159 says what part 2 does to learn how far the extrapolation can be trusted (a long soak of an hour as an experiment, not as a gate).

**Alternatives considered.** Printing "projected drift over three hours" on the sheet (a number that sounds like a measurement and is a multiplication of the least reliable quantity of the run). A longer release soak (30 minutes is about what one job holds a runner for, `criteria.ts`).

**Consequences.** The checklist's soak item asks for the release soak in every engine and says "CI soak only" for a shorter one. The strategy's own words about the limits of the soak stay true and are now backed by what the evaluators refuse to do.

**Defending it.** "I wrote down what a thirty-minute run can and cannot find before I ran any, so that the result is read against an honest scope: it finds the defects that show in thirty minutes, and it says nothing it cannot."

## ADR-159: How the criteria are validated, what part 1 checked, and what is left to part 2

**Context.** Every limit of `LIVE_READINESS` is initial policy. The brief for part 2 is to compare, for each limit, the measured distribution with the limit, to revise the ones that are wrong with the evidence (too tight: healthy runs fail; too loose: a real defect passes), to change their basis to `measured` naming the committed artifact, and to report a limit that the defaults cannot meet as a finding.

**Decision, part 1 (done).** The evaluators and the procedures were checked without a browser, and **mutation-checked**: a defect was put into the code, the readiness unit tests were run (one worker), the failing tests were read, and the edit was reverted (the diff of `src` was empty each time).

| --- | --- |
| `judge`: the limit made exclusive (`<` instead of `<=`) | 27 |
| `judge`: the direction of the comparison flipped | 67 |
| `fitLine`: the sign of the slope flipped | 6 |
| `pooledSlope`: the sign of the pooled slope flipped | 4 |
| the drift judged without its absolute value (a falling latency passes) | 1 |
| `restoreFrom`: the maximum made exclusive | 2 |
| the rebuffer ratio counts the injected outage | 2 |
| one line fitted through both sides of an outage instead of one slope with two levels | 2 |

Two of them survived, or were caught by too little, at first, which is what the table is for. The heap-baseline mutation was caught only by a test about the number of readings, because the test about the warm-up used a startup allocation (10 MB) smaller than the limit: it was rewritten with a startup allocation of 90 MB and the mutation is now caught by it as well (2 failed). The exclusion of manifest gaps survived completely, because the one gap in the test lay entirely inside its window, which "overlaps" and "contains" both exclude: a window that only touches a gap, and one that ends where a gap starts, were added, and the mutation is now caught (1 failed). The synthetic series include a flat one, a drifting one (both signs), a leaking one, a spiking one, an empty one and one that is too short; the line fits and the drift are also checked under noise, with 300 seeds each and one verdict for all of them. **What the fake of Playwright found (Phase A.2).** The adapters (page, world, soak driver) and the two commands were run for the first time over the fake of ADR-150, and the real `--help` of both commands was run. They found **no defect in the adapters**, and two defects in text: the usage of `npm run readiness` said that every configuration decides the exit code, where the code and CONTRACTS section 9.2 say that only the build as it ships does (fixed, and the usage test pins it), and the sheet's line for the relative reading had a label longer than its column (shortened). They also showed that `playwrightWorld` takes its clock from `performance.now()`, which is right for a run and made the first version of the test hang for the 65 s of real time that the stall check's observation is bounded by: the world and the driver now take a clock, and the tests give them the virtual one. What the fake cannot find is listed in ADR-150.

**Decision, part 2 (to do, in this order).** (1) The readiness checks in every engine through the command (`npm run readiness -- --candidate live-sync`: Chromium and macOS WebKit natively, Firefox and Linux WebKit through `npm run docker:pw -- --cpus 3`), serially, on a quiet machine, results committed under `docs/results/readiness`. (2) The CI soak in Chromium (and the others if time allows) and one release soak, results under `docs/results/soak`, with the five-minute dry run of the harness's memory first. (3) For every limit, the measured distribution against the limit: a limit that healthy runs approach (inside, say, the run-to-run spread) is too tight; a limit far above anything measured, with a defect that the check would pass, is too loose, and the defect is *injected on purpose* to find out (a slow link for the join, a media clock that runs at 0.99 for the drift, a page that retains a megabyte a second for the heap, an outage of 20 s so that the unrestored latency is far above 10 s, instead of at it). (4) Revise in `criteria.ts` with the evidence, change the basis to `measured` naming the artifact, regenerate the strategy's table (`npm run docs:check -- --write`). The numbers already known to be loose from the live specs are the 8 s p95 (the latency at the first frame was 6.1 to 7.2 s), the 10 s maximum (6.2 to 7.0 s over 20 s windows) and the 5 s start (about a second, 1.6 s at the worst, in Linux WebKit in Docker). (5) PB-RDY-003 for A stays a NO-GO unless the evidence changes; it is a finding, and the report carries it with the candidate's numbers next to it. (6) **Choose the discriminator of PB-RDY-003** (ADR-153): with the measured values of both readings, in every engine, for A and for the candidate, decide which of the absolute limit and the reading relative to the session's own level separates a player that came back from one that did not, with the wider margin and fewer false calls, and whether the tolerance of one segment is the right one; no limit is changed before that data exists. (7) **Look at the two estimators of the drift on real series** (ADR-156): how often Theil-Sen and least squares differ by more than the limit, what the series looks like when they do, and whether a disagreement should make the line INCONCLUSIVE; inject a late climb on purpose (a media clock that speeds up for the last quarter) to see what the note shows. (8) The decision on the errors that no injected fault explains is the owner's (ADR-156), not part 2's; part 2 only reports how many a real soak has. This record is where the results of part 2 are written.

**Alternatives considered.** Setting the limits from the first batch of measurements (a distribution from one machine on one afternoon is the first sample, not the policy: part 2 repeats before it revises). Not revising a limit that no healthy run fails (a limit above any defect that matters is as wrong as one below healthy variation; the injected defects are how that is told).

**Consequences.** Until part 2 ships, a green PB-RDY-001, 002 or 004 says "within the initial policy", and the sheet's header says the limits are those of `criteria.ts` as committed.

**Defending it.** "A validation that cannot fail is not a validation, so the evaluators were broken on purpose and the tests were read when they did not notice. The limits will be judged the same way: by whether a real defect, put in on purpose, crosses them."
